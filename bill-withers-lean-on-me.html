Student writing · Track 03

Bill Withers — Lean On Me

STUDENT WRITINGOpen original · Google Docs

The 1972 hit “Lean on Me” by Bill Withers is a song expressing the value of community. Withers wrote the song after moving to Los Angeles for his career aspirations, longing for the community ethic of his hometown of Slab Fork, West Virginia (Mahncke 2023).

The song features a simple and commonly beloved C, F, G, C chord progression on the piano, responded to by higher-pitched accent notes (first heard at 0:04 and 0:07). Withers hums the melody of the first verse before singing, setting the tone for a heartfelt soul piece. The song is played in 4/4 time, also known as “common time”, with percussive accents on beats 2 and 4. I believe this was an intentional stylistic choice Withers made. Minimizing the melodic and rhythmic complexity of the piece allows for the listener to focus on the lyrical content of the verse.

Withers’ timbre begins rich and mellow, as he tells a story in the first verse, “sometimes in our lives / we all have pain / we all have sorrow / but if we are wise / we know that there’s always tomorrow”. As he begins the chorus, he moves into his upper register, sounding more bright and nasal as he calls on his audience to have the courage to ask for help from a friend. The second verse omits the soulful piano chords and relies on percussion to drive Withers’ message. Withers is joined by a chorus at 2:03, metaphorically signifying the importance of community, something Withers lacked at the time this song was written. The second chorus is accompanied by strings, adding gospel elements to the piece. At 2:58, piano chords return, paying homage to the first verse. Here, Withers sings the same melody as the beginning of the piece, but with a brighter and prouder tone, signifying his triumph over hardship. The piece ends with a call and response between Withers and his choir, “if you need a friend (call me) / if you need a friend (call me)” as the song fades.

While I can’t recall the first time I ever heard this song, I remember hearing this song at a local outdoor concert in Exton, PA while I was visiting my cousin and her family for a weekend during the summer before 7th grade. When the local band played this song, everyone in the audience stopped their picnic and conversation to sing along. I recall this as being my first extramusical experience. People of all ages and identities knew the song, showing me the importance of music as a tool to establish community.
