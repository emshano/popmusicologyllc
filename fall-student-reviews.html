For my song response, I decided to analyze “Ball & Chain” by Big Mama Thornton because I really enjoy expressive guitar work and felt it was an interesting combination of classic blues with some rock influence. I am a big fan of indie and classic rock and also have a background in Jazz, and I could hear themes of all three in this performance.

Thornton’s vocal timbre is definitely my favorite part of the song, as she displays a rich tonal variety, with a number of powerful growls and rasps, making the delivery especially expressive. She exudes strength, and I’m incredibly impressed and moved by the vulnerability required to perform a song about the heavy burden of loving someone who mistreats you. Such a message was uncommon at a time when songwriters tended to write about more frivolous subjects suited to the conventional male gaze. This context makes Thornton’s performance even more powerful and uniquely vulnerable.

Musically, the slow, easy tempo and swing rhythm contribute to the feeling of being “weighed down,” like the “ball & chain” weighing her down in the lyrics. The song also features simple instrumentation, allowing her voice to take center stage, ultimately giving the performance a much more personal feel. Her voice and the guitar feed off each other in a call-and-response pattern, typical of the blues, making the song feel more conversational, more personal, and, for that, more vulnerable. The pauses between lines are also particularly expressive and heart-wrenching as they convey a sense of struggle in delivering the lyrics, emphasizing the weight of the themes in the song.

While I also liked Janice Joplin’s version, I found her interpretation to be sharper and angrier, with heavy, loud guitar, and piercing screams. Conversely, Big Mama Thornton’s performance conveys both strength and sorrow, making it more personal and, in my opinion, powerful.

I think this song is best listened to, as Mama Thornton puts it: “Sittin’ by my window… lookin’ out at the rain.” It’s a song that requires introspection and reminds us of the importance and strength required to be vulnerable. It also sends a powerful message about the difficulty, but also the necessity, of walking away from those who mistreat you.

This is the version I listened to— definitely worth a listen!

I chose to write about “Sweet Home Chicago” by Robert Johnson, recorded in 1936. I initially chose this song from the playlist for a personal reason, which is that I love listening to songs that reference cities and real-life places. From acoustic pop hits like “Manhattan” by Sara Bareilles (2012) to TV show theme songs like “California (The O.C.)” by Phantom Planet (2006), these types of location-based songs help connect for me what otherwise might feel more like a third party's personal story to more tangible physical spaces that I can visualize. A few years ago, I actually made a playlist of city-based songs for fun.

However, unlike the aforementioned examples, the lyrics of “Sweet Home Chicago” do not serve to describe the titular location of this song. In fact, the lyrical voice/narrator seems to singing from elsewhere in the country, begging his “baby” (0:04) to join him in leaving for Chicago, “the land of California” (0:25), and “Des Moines, Iowa” (2:30). In the context of the Great Migration (Brice), which was still ongoing at the time of this song, one could believe that this is in reference to the movement of Black people in the late 19th and early 20th centuries out of the Jim Crow South and into other regions of the country. Upon further research, some argue that he actually may be singing about the industrial town of Port Chicago in northern California (McClelland). Nonetheless, the idealization of such places far away from the south affirms the inherent Blackness of the narrator's experience, which in turn, adds to the bluesy feeling of both this song and musical genre. This interpretation of the lyrics makes even more sense considering that the Mississippi-born Johnson spent his recording career exclusively in the south (Britannica).

Port Chicago is also situated about an hour from my hometown in the Oakland area, which adds to my personal enjoyment of this particular song – as I only know of Port Chicago from the “Port Chicago Highway” that I’ve driven on a few times, I had no idea that a town used to exist there until this song inspired me to do some additional digging (McClelland). While these potential references to Port Chicago are by no means a musical element by themselves, they add to the historical retrospective that this song naturally creates when listened to in 2024. This song's place is no longer in popular music, but as an artifact of a past American perspective.

Many quintessential elements typical in blues songs appear throughout this track. Johnson’s voice is flexible in both pitch and rhythm, with no vocal harmony to support him. One hears mainly Johnson’s chest voice (e.g. at 2:12), as he sings in a forte volume in many parts of this song. I like that the “shouting” timbre and flexibility of his vocals create a casual, imprecise tone — a conversational feeling that also speaks to the blues’ roots in fieldwork songs sung outdoors. Even when the same lyrics are sung multiple times, the small sonic alterations that Johnson makes allow the song to not feel overly repetitive (e.g. the singing of “To my sweet home Chicago” at 1:24 is much more legato, with the syllables connected and held for a longer duration compared to its prior appearance at 0:58). Upon repeated listens, I notice more small syncopations (0:23) and irregular singing patterns (1:04), which together create the swing rhythm that keeps the song feeling alive and interesting throughout (at least for me).

The melody of the chorus also starts at a high pitch (e.g. “Baby, don’t you want to go?”), then ends much lower to give a sense of melodic closure (“Chicago”). Furthermore, the chorus follows, more or less, the AAB structure commonplace in this genre, with the refrain “Back to the land of California / To my sweet home Chicago” (0:54) serving as a very catchy “B” phrase — as this song has progressively become stuck in my head, it is principally this phrase that I have heard on repeat.

The production of this song — simple vocals atop acoustic strumming — speak to both the song’s place of origin in the south and time period of the 1930s, given that the electric blues began slightly later around the 1940s and more as a result of blues regionalisms (e.g. the Chicago blues). This track serves as a great example many elements of what one could consider to be an older, deeply southern blues song and a narrative of Black self-invention. While I do not foresee it topping my Spotify Wrapped anytime soon, I enjoyed it for its encapsulation of history — reading about its possible subjects and references to the Great Migration made this a fun assignment for me.

I chose to review the song “Waiting for a Train” by Jimmie Rodgers, who sang in his signature “blue yodel” and is sometimes known as the “Father of Country Music”. I watched a performance of this song from the 1929 short film “The Singing Brakeman”, where Jimmie Rodgers sits in front of a railroad station building and performs three songs.

“Waiting for a Train” is performed on a single guitar and vocals, and recounts the story of a penniless man trying to return back home but instead getting kicked off the train. This type of “hillbilly music” targeted the poor, white, adult working class and often had themes of hardship and suffering. The song follows a basic 12-bar blues structure and the instrumental part is not very complex, allowing others to easily sing along. It tells the daily struggles of a poor man trying to find work, which would have been especially relatable for many people during the Great Depression in the 1930s. The tempo is set almost at a speaking speed, giving the sense that Rodgers is languidly telling us his story. Two women are also in the scene, one drying dishes and one sewing an article of clothing, further giving the performance an impression of candidness. Rodgers starts off with a simple guitar intro and then imitates a train whistle, using his mouth to create a cool vocal sound effect (0:11). After the intro, he sings the first verse (0:27), yodels (1:10), sings the second verse (1:19), then yodels again to end the song (2:00). His signature blue yodel feels slower than the rest of the song despite being at the same tempo because the yodeling does not fill the entire space. There are short periods of vocal silence while the guitar strums rhythmically, giving the listener a calm rest before the next verse.

I actually discovered this song on Youtube back when I was in elementary school. I initially liked this song a lot because the tune was catchy yet melancholy, and the lyrics were sad. It was also not too musically-complex for a regular person to sing. As a kid, I would sing this song whenever I was homesick. After listening to it again now, I still like the song, and I appreciate the simplicity of the performance. It’s clearly performative, but it also just feels like a guy singing with a guitar.

“Over The Rainbow,” performed by Judy Garland, was released in 1939 during the Tin Pan Alley music era. Because it was featured in The Wizard of Oz, a prominent American film, the song has reached a broad audience that spans generations and remains popular today. The lyrics describe a heaven-like place where “the dreams that you dare to dream really do come true” (1:06) and where “troubles melt like lemon drops” (1:29). When Garland sings, “that’s where you’ll find me” (1:34), she implies this place is not an unattainable utopia, but a hope for the future. Thus, this song is likely an attempt to help the listener imagine a positive future, specifically during times of personal or widespread hardship. Garland’s emotional performance reinforces this idea as her voice sounds like she is longing to escape her current life to go to the place she is singing about. This theme is particularly significant given that the song was released towards the end of the Great Depression.

Like many songs written during the Tin Pan Alley era, “Over The Rainbow” follows AABA musical form. Although the three A sections (0:31, 0:53, 1:43) have different lyrics after the words “somewhere over the rainbow,” they share the same melody. The B section, which begins with “someday I’ll wish upon a star” (1:15), has a different melody than the A sections and includes a predominantly faster tempo. The melodies in the song are mostly disjunct because they are hard to sing and use many skips and leaps (wide intervals between adjacent notes). In addition, the song has a homophonic texture since it features a prominent melody—sung by Garland—and a less complex accompaniment—played by various instruments.

While this song no longer aligns with my music preferences because it is slow and not energetic, I used to love it when I was younger. As a little girl, I admired Judy Garland’s character in The Wizard of Oz, so I sang “Over the Rainbow” whenever possible. I remember selecting it as my voice recital song in elementary school and singing it with my best friend during a karaoke birthday party. Now, I appreciate Garland’s ability to beautifully sing the jumpy melody and the hopeful theme of the lyrics. Sadly, I can imagine this song being played at a funeral, particularly for people who believe in an afterlife and that death is a passage to a better place.

Muddy Waters’ performance of “I’m Your Hoochie Coochie Man” in 1954 is one of many that solidifies him as the “Father of Chicago Blues.” Composed and written by Willie Dixon to match Waters’ persona as an artist, the piece boasts a sense of narcissism and sexual appeal within the lyrics, heightened by African-American spiritualism. Lines like “ You were born for good luck,” (3:12) and “he gonna make pretty women's, jump and shout” (0:18) all contribute to this idea that he was destined for greatness when it came to attracting women. This fortune Waters sings of stems from “Hoodoo,” spiritual practices and traditions originating among the enslaved in the South and referenced at the start of the second verse: “I got a black cat bone, I got a mojo too

” (0:57).

To complement the spirituality hidden behind Waters’ radiating confidence, the composition follows a steady, adagio tempo, performed at around 75pm in a compound quadruple meter. While Dixon still abides by the standard form of the Blues using the tonic, sub-dominant, and dominant 7th chords, this song follows a 16-bar progression with 4 extra bars of the chord in the song’s key, A major. As to not feel too repetitive however, the harmonica, bass, piano, guitar, and drums all play a conjunct, syncopated riff, first heard at the beginning of the track and throughout the three verses to serve as the “response” to Waters’ “call.” Furthermore, the song's performance style is both instrumentally and vocally expressive. The improvisation of the guitar (1:48) is more disjunct and adds to the strings’ unique timbre, while Waters’ transitions between speaking normally “(1:06) and his emotionally belting (1:27) emphasize both the alluring and spiritual nature of the song.

While the song isn’t one I’d listen to daily, I’ve still come to appreciate the sheer talent showcased with something new after every listen. As someone who’s played a Blues chord progression on the piano in the past, I’ve grown especially fond of the “response” riff and the cadence played descendingly at every turnaround (3:47). Moreover, in the rap scene today with incessant sampling and it’s popular themes of self-esteem and hypersexuality, I can easily see Waters’ recording of lyrics like “I’m him” and“Coochie man” on the next BossMan Dlow, Rob49, or GloRilla track trending on Tik Tok.

I chose to review “A Most Unusual Day” by Beverly Kenney for my musical reflection. To begin, let’s delve into the historical context of the piece. Written in the Tin Pan Alley (TPA) era, this jazz song utilizes the AABA song form with a walking baseline that propels melodic movement. While Beverly’s rendition is from the Jazz era in the 1950s, the song was written during the Tin Pan Alley era. The song alludes to the day being unusual, emphasizing elements of sunshine, human connection, and love blossoming in the vocalist’s day to day life. This alludes to the vocalist being in love, and how this sensation deviates from her norm. Not only is this lyrically expressed, but it is also embedded within the arrangement itself. The deviant vocal melody line and musical jumps in the baseline that so clearly convey standard jazz music elements stray greatly from the norm of Tin Pan Alley ballads, where they are usually slower and have more of an emphasis on the main vocalist. Beverly Kenney sings with a very light, crooning style, which conveys a feeling, to me, of beauty in small moments. At Penn, I am the music director for Counterparts, a group that specializes in pop and jazz a cappella, and whenever we sing jazz music in this style, we target a vibe of carefree vocalizing that purely conveys emotions of bliss. Beverly Kenney has done just that in her recording of “A Most Unusual Day” and the vocal slides and slight vibrato she adds at the end of her sustained notes further contributes towards this overall energy in the song.

Delving further into the musical elements, the polyphony within the arrangement attracts listener attention to each song element, rather than just the vocal melody, which adds depth to the arrangement and message of the song. The first two stanzas are quite predictable in terms of chord structure, which juxtaposes the message of the day being unusual, however in the B section the chord structure and melody deviates before returning to the predictable A section. After the third A section, there is another B followed by more A sections and a short instrumental section at the end of the piece. There are also slight moments within the piece that stray from the original A melody line, specifically from 0:44-0:51 when Kenney sings the minor third above the regular melody for “it’s a most unusual time”. This strays from what the ear expects, and when Kenney does it again at around 1:12 until 1:17, it is more easily anticipated because the listener has already heard the deviation. This element, in my mind, serves as a metaphor on how musicians during this period began to deviate in their creative processes from the societal standards at the time, both artistically and culturally.

Moving into my emotional reaction individual preferences, this song reminds me of a fresh, spring day and the new beginnings that come with it. When overlaying my own emotions with those of the vocalist, there is a clear parallel between the feelings of love that Kenney is feeling and the rebirth of love and appreciation for beautiful weather that I feel in the springtime. This song also reminds me to let myself relax and experience the sensation of being carefree. So often at Penn, I worry about the pressures of academic deadlines and responsibilities, and this song brings light and refreshing energy into my life. This song also aligns with my preferences for jazz. I’ve been performing at Birdland Jazz Club in New York since I was twelve, and this style of music always brings me back to the special memories I have there. This song is a staple on my holiday playlist, as well as on my springtime playlist. While I recognize that many people today tend to select more contemporary songs for such playlists, I believe this song should continue to be played in various jazz clubs and environments across the country and the world in the 2020s. Its classic jazz form and chord structure is nostalgia-inducing, and the message is still quite relevant today.

For this Song review, I chose to review “Lean on Me” by Bill Withers. Released in 1972 as the lead single from his upcoming album, Still Bill, Withers used the song in order to reminisce and offer appreciation for his upbringing in the rural south and show praise for the community and strength he’d witnessed there. At the same time, Withers had just moved to Los Angeles, indicating his own struggle to find place and comfort in his environment. Beyond his own narrative, the song’s story also reflects the tensions and struggles in the United States at the time (Mahncke, 2023). The Civil Rights Movement - famously the campaign to abolish legalized “racial segregation, discrimination, and disenfranchisement” in the US - had officially ended in 1968 (Library of Congress, 2024). Thus, “Lean on Me” offered hope and support for the country at a time of heightened inequality, turmoil, and tension

The lyrics of the song demonstrate the vitality of unity and support for one another. In the chorus, Withers sings “Lean on me, when you’re not strong/And I’ll be your friend/… For it won’t be long/Till I’m gonna need somebody to lean on” (0:52-1:18). The message of the song is clearly one of support for others and to do unto others as you’d have them do unto you. Essentially, Withers is echoing the need to be there for and care for your neighbors as in times of struggle and tension such as in the US at the time, there is an amplified need for unity.

Beyond just the lyrics, Withers delivers his story through the instrumentation and vocal delivery just as effectively. From the opening of the song, Withers’ voice is sincere and calming, almost as though he is reassuring the listeners that things will turn out alright. With the use of a repeating chord progression in C, F, G, C with a response of accent notes, Withers offers an intentionally simple, familiar, and warm instrumentation coupled with heartfulness and fullness in his vocal timbre to set a tone of openness and kindness in the song. As the song progresses, there are instruments added which amplify the message that Withers is sharing about community. For instance, the gospel components of a chorus being added act almost as a metaphor for the community and support Withers is calling for, something that I believe was an intentional choice. It seems as though the implementation of the chorus at 2:05, in the middle of the song, acts as a triumph, an assurance that community will be there and come about when you need them most.

Having attended a Catholic school from Kindergarten through 8th grade, I had to sing this song alongside my classmates for our 3rd grade Christmas show. I remember being so touched by the lyrics and finding them to be empowering when singing them with my friends of 5 years or more at that point. I think it is very communal and important to hear and it brings me joy to have been exposed to it so long ago.

The song that I chose is “(I’m Your) Hoochie Coochie Man” (1954) by Muddy Waters. This song is played in a classic Chicago blues style, a style of Urban blues inspired by Delta blues which features guitar and harmonica as the prominent instruments. This song is also one of the first to use the “stop-time” arrangement, which means that the instrumental pauses after every measure while Muddy fills in with his vocals.

The song's lyrics are rooted in themes of fortune, sexual desire, and the supernatural. It speaks of lyrics such as “he gonna make pretty womens jump and shout” and “I’m gonna make you girls lead me by my hand.” It also mentions a black cat bone and a mojo, which are hoodoo charms. Hoodoo is a collection of religious traditions that were practiced by enslaved African Americans. Willie Dixon was the songwriter of this piece and he gave the song to Waters because he felt that it was right for him. I am glad they both agreed to incorporate those meaningful cultural elements into their music.

The song is written in a sixteen-bar blues style, an extended version of the commonly used twelve-bar blues style. The song has a polyphonic texture with each instrument playing a distinct melody. Additionally, it follows a slow and steady tempo (adagio). The clear rhythmic pattern makes the song easy to listen to and tap your foot along to the beat. I love the squeal of the harmonica throughout the song and how the piano compliments its melody to create close harmonies. The electric guitar is the most prominent in the middle of the song (1:48). The guitar adds a richer, sexier feel to the music and its presence in the second half really drives the song home. Another thing I adore about this piece is the contrast between the darker timbre of Muddy’s vocals and the higher timbre of the other instruments.

Era/Theme: The Civil Rights Movement and Girl Groups

The 1966 hit song “You Can’t Hurry Love” by The Supremes serves as a prime example of the Motown machine and its promotion of the respectable female ideal. Despite the track’s popularity then, I believe it holds much less relevance today in the face of contemporary popular music.

Vocally, the three voices of the The Supremes — at least, in this iteration of the group — are energetic and casual (0:25), similar to the doo wop styles of decades prior (Victoria and Albert Museum). As opposed to Big Mama Thornton or Adele, The Supremes sing high-pitched, fast-moving melodies that harmonize well with each other (1:43). Throughout the song, and especially in the chorus, there are numerous instances of call and response between Ross and the other Supremes (0:25, 0:31, 1:49, etc.), in which the other singers add small quips onto the main lyric. However, Ross maintains control over the main melody, with the “responses” being quite subtle background harmonies. Overall, the vocals come across as conversational and imperfect, with Ross varying her pitches and rhythms when she repeats a musical / lyrical phrase (2:08). She takes some liberties by singing a few runs (e.g. 0:08, “I need love, loooooove to ease my mind”) but falls short of ad-libbing lyrics or deviating too much from the expected melody. The vocals sound simultaneously informal yet meticulously planned.

The instrumentation is defined by an upbeat bass line, high-pitched percussion (almost like a tambourine), and a syncopated trumpet blare. Compared to, say, blues music from earlier in the century, this song involves more complexity in its instrumentation, as it involves multiple instruments and a high amount of coordination. Nonetheless, there is nothing electronic or overly modern here — a small band would likely be able to provide all the components of the instrumental track. Although developments in recording technology enabled the “wall of sound” to be invented around this time, this instrumentation remains quite simplistic.

Together, these elements help build the feminine sound crafted by Motown, as detailed in the later “historical elements” section. These female voices are cheery and light, singing easily accessible lyrics about love.
