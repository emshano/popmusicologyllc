Kwame Kruw Ocran

Comprehensive Exam, Topic II

Public Musicology

When the New York Times goes agog over a Billy Joel conference, I see a sign for more applied, or public musicological work and community engagement.[1] Spaces and thought have been invested in public musicology as a form of community engagement. Such public endeavors celebrate musical subjects, not simply for intellectual kin, but with non-specialist audiences in mind; the origin, maturation and development of this discipline merits discussion.

With news coverage comes attention towards a public musicology enjoying massive popularity between academics and enthusiasts alike. But this was not always so. For the longest time, such events have been seen as mere offshoots of musicology that emphasize an applied ethic in knowledge production and practice, much to the chagrin of “pure” scholarship adherents. Others chalk public musicology as a type of public applied scholarship because it dedicates all its fruitful products to the commerce of public ideas. This comprehensive exam answer differs, however. The scholarship comprising the following review of literature speaks to its own discipline that combines knowledge production and intentional community engagement.

Seeking its foundation and evolution through textual readings, I hope to survey the field of public musicology through defining developments. From its emergence as an earlier form of “applied” musicology, through to recent interrogations into public musicological work and intention, the discipline’s primary contours will offer insights into its trajectory as well as its current obstacles and future solutions. A review of recent scholarship on the field will establish a working definition set aside from other approaches to public applied scholarship, namely music criticism or public intellectualism. Afterwards, a meditation on past theorizations of public and applied musicology will assist in tracing the intellectual development of public musicological discourse. Following discussion of public musicology’s foundations, we will revisit the discipline at present to examine contemporary definitions, applications and rationale. To close, I will offer future implications toward the discipline based on insights developed through close readings of public musicology’s past and present.

What is Public Musicology?

To define public musicology, several publications provide foundational understandings for a wide-ranging discipline that continues to transform in the present. The foundation is then further defined by examining public musicology vis-à-vis other forms of public applied scholarship—music criticism, and public intellectualism.[2]

Definitions of public musicology tend to vary, denoting an intellectual gesture to multiple, diverse audiences. Reba A. Wissner’s “A Review of Public Musicology” provides a broad conception that provides formal flexibility. Advocating variety in form and practice, Wissner only requires that public musicologists “activate [their] knowledge of music history and culture in order to communicate information about music effectively, whether to novices or experts.”[3] Her emphasis on engaging the public is apt because it leaves form to the practitioner’s discretion. Yet, her citation of educator Gert Biesta is quite prescriptive, offering three types of public engagement: “pedagogy for the public, pedagogy of the public, and pedagogy that enacts concern for publicness.”[4] (emphasis mine) Concentrated on publics, Wissner’s list of forms is quite expansive; several activities qualify as public musicology, including: podcasting, livestreaming, (accessible) blogging for the public, trade books, as well as traditional platforms such as music landmarks preservation, film and documentary consulting, and forensic musicology. To perform public musicology, Wissner advances the practice of storytelling to frame works “for the reader, to the reader, or as a concern for the reader.”[5]

Jack Buehrer’s NYT reporting on the Billy Joel conference is an example of public musicology that tells Joel’s story to the reader by framing related musical ideas as a pedagogy of the public.[6] Not every endeavor in the name of public musicology fits neatly into Wissner’s framework, however. “Don Giovanni Goes to Prison: Teaching Opera Behind Bars” sees opera scholar Pierpaolo Polzonetti reflect on his experiences teaching the Mozart opera to the incarcerated. If we were to dovetail Wissner’s framework onto Polzonetti, the latter presents public musicology that enacts concern for publicness; he suggests an indispensability in professional musicologists as caretakers for non-specialists.[7] His storytelling is framed directly to the reader—however, this time, his intended audience is actually the intellectual community of musicologists.

Beyond dissonance with Wissner, Polzonetti’s post and perspective produced seismic interrogations of the discipline and its intentions at the time. Particularly, Bonnie Gordon’s response, “The Perils of Public Musicology,” questioned the parameters of the discipline writ large in a way that also conceptualized additional standards for public musicological work. She decries Polzonetti’s “helpful” musicologist as paternalistic, advancing a standard of inclusion. Gordon celebrates her own program as one that uses “institutional resources to collaboratively (as in: with the community) address and solve challenge facing communities.”[8]

She also sees making mistakes as part of the occupation, taking the occasion of “Don Giovanni” to remind scholars to act with intention and with an eye for known unknowns. In the same breath, she emphasizes the community engagement aspect of public musicology, one she finds lacking in Polzenetti.[9] Questioning the discipline’s gatekeeping through classical tradition, Gordon sees public musicology challenging hegemony to democratize valuable repertoires and representations of knowledge alongside communities.

Public Intellectualism and Music Criticism

The discipline registers as applied public scholarship that emphasizes the effective communication of musicological and cultural information, conceived with an intention of organic community engagement. Defined here by Wissner, Polzenetti and Gordon, and exemplified by Buehrer, public musicology mildly contrasts with other defined forms of applied public scholarship, namely public intellectualism and music criticism.

English scholar Helen Small’s conception of the public intellectual is cited here for capturing a kindred spirit of upheaval like that inspired by Polzenetti, Gordon, and others. The call for intentional community engagement in musicology is somewhat like the anxieties Small recognized in her reflection of the field, attempting to speak to its changing terrain.[10] Similarly theorized as translators, both are considered “custodians of cultural values,” yet musicologists center music and related cultures and practices in their work. Both tend to emphasize multiculturalism.[11] Though capable of musical endeavors, public intellectuals are not beholden to communities to engage musically; intellectuals are expected to exert “pressure for the audience to desire.”[12] Further, public intellectuals enter the public arena from multiple vocations (freelancers, journalists, tenured intellectuals[13]), yet professional public musicologists tend to be academics; Wissner, however, acknowledges that one becomes a public musicologist “from the moment you pick up your instrument or begin singing.”[14]

This is mind, both feature a fragmentation in expertise within the public sphere, due to the porous nature of what Small calls “new media.”[15] Both intellectuals and musicologists use a myriad of methods to disseminate information. Small could not have predicted the ubiquity of social media in 2002, but she accurately identified that “technologies such as the Web and email increasingly permit people to move in and out of different ‘knowledge situations’ in which they have widely varying degrees of expertise and influence. In short, they may operate as public intellectuals in some public contexts, while in others they will have no claim to intellectual authority at all.”[16] The practical murkiness of the public sphere online aside, Small’s mandate for meaningful action from intellectuals as organizers can be seen as redundant in public musicology since the latter orients itself to practice and community engagement.[17]

Likewise, a narrowly defined music criticism may share thematic similarities with public musicology, the former cannot eclipse the latter. Music critics, like public musicologists, have faced deep ethical concerns—instead of paternalism and elitism, a mitigation of greed for money and influence.[18] Notably, criticism has directly facilitated social mobility through an education of the public and informing public taste; this differs from musicological and intentional community engagement.[19]

Lars Helgert’s contribution to Grove Music Online defines the tradition primarily through “dissemination, intended readership, and nature of its content.”[20] A publication-bounded practice, music criticism usually appears as “relative brief writings in serial publications intended for general readerships that evaluate the quality of musical performance and or composition.”[21] Given Helgert’s relatively strict interpretation of music criticism, its journalistic tendency in America, its appearance in the form of the concert review, the extended essay (think piece), or the record review, a public musicologist could likely function as a music critic.[22] However, the music critic is bounded to write by her traditional forms; to work away from “writing reviews in closer temporal proximity to release” in America is to engage music from a different perspective.[23] Further, American public musicological training, cited by Wissner, maintains a relative stronghold when juxtaposed with noted failed attempts to academize music criticism throughout the twentieth century; Helgert highlights that critics of the rock tradition, for example, valorize DIY independent scholarship as authentic as opposed to formal university training.

Public musicology, public intellectualism, and music criticism, though related as applied public forms of scholarship, are distinct insofar as each is concerned with a form of public-minded education. What distinguishes the former is its insistence on functioning toward empowering and engaging communities, which tends to a welcomed sense of intellectual humility. The following section reviews the development of public musicological thought from the late nineteenth through the twenty-first centuries.

Past Theorizations of Public Musicology

Scholarship concerning the origins and development of public musicology trace the discipline’s flowering from its fledgling period in the late 19th century through its formalization during the 20th, and to continued questions and disciplinary challenges in the twenty-first century. This section accomplishes several objectives: to locate early applied theories as well as progenitive thought around public engagement, to recognize the disciplinary calls for impact beyond musicology, to recapitulate arguments by Richard Taruskin and Phillip Gossett critiquing historically-informed performance, to examine the methodological incursions of New Musicology in Susan McClary’s “Constructions of Subjectivity in Schubert’s Music,” to revisit terminological nuances Jeff Todd Titon established in relation to scope with regards to applied ethnomusicology for the interest of the public, and finally, to celebrate 1939 as a pivotal year that saw questions raised that still reckon with public musicology nearly ninety years later.

Early Applied Musicologies

Malik Sharif’s article is a disciplinary history beginning from the debate of “pure” versus “applied” notions of musicology and ending with debates over degrees of application, arguing from the outset, “musicology was applied,” and identifying four foundational thinkers to modern applied musicologies.[24] (emphasis mine) Anxious over establishing musicology’s reputation, Phillip Spitta lectured against its application in 1883, advocating a scientific, positivist rigor.[25] Guido Adler, who appears in Sharif, Zapletal & Dromey, and Loges, countered Spitta two years later expounding the relationship between practice and musicological scholarship and advocating a relatively wider view of supportive musicologists as cultural guardians.[26] By 1903, Hermann Kretzschmar[27] theorized applied musicology away from individual self-realization and towards the preservation of state and society.[28]

Sharif indicates modern applied musicology beginning to take familiar form during the mid-1930s and the work of Charles Seeger, who, by estimate of the previously mentioned, offered broader applications of applied musicology beyond music. Believing that musicologists should adopt a narrow sense of applied work, Seeger advocated for other vocations including music education, governmental work, and (inter)national cultural policy advocacy, sharing commonality with Adler and Kretzschmar.[29] Sharif considers contemporary public musicology to carry Adler’s vision of musicological custodianship as well as Seeger’s highly-applicable sense of applied ethnomusicology, but finds Kretzschmar’s thought of a highly organized musical life fleeting.

Consonant with early combinations of scholarship and application, Zapletal and Dromey’s account of the connection between scholarship, music-making, and musealization or “the effort to extract certain cultural elements in which value is recognized to preserve and use further in new ways evidence,” is instrumental to musicology.[30] Concerned with musicalia, musical and related material texts, the authors attribute the work of Giambattista Martini and the Central European School of Music Historiography as causes musicology’s birth through their serious reading of musicalia from 1750 onwards and especially during the 19th century.[31] Preservation proved significant to the foregrounding of systematic archival research at this time, away from the creation of composer biographies, and toward a shift to a positivistic professionalism.[32]

Natasha Loges celebrates the liminality of shifting strategies in Austro-German performance of the 19th century concert to suggest their diversity and heterogeneity as a corrective to established musicological hierarchies.[33] Her rendering of concerts aligns with Sharif, and Zapletal & Dromey insofar as Adler’s “idealistic” vision of a measurable, comparable, and classifiable musicology is pronounced in scholarship.[34] She claims the concert as the “ultimate” form of public musicology over music criticism, because of its higher reach towards strategies of repertoire, and its expression of value.[35] Where Loges studied the concert, Bruno Bower discussed public-facing implications of program notes, essays printed in concert programs to explain music to audiences, and presented them as a clear example of the application of musicological ideas.[36] Further, he makes serious mention of George Grove, whose dictionary was equally foregrounding.[37] November’s work, though not temporally-bound, neatly concludes the pure versus applied debate began by Spitta and Adler in Sharif. Considering phenomenology, the study of lived experiences, perceptions and feelings, and practice-led research, knowledge production as or through creative practice, she sees the two existing in cyclical symbiosis, likening the relationship to a reflexivity much like the scientific method.[38]

Last, Paul Henry Lang’s “Musicology and Musical Letters” articulates an early charge to engage general audiences legibly. Writing in 1968, his critique of narrow-minded specialization encouraged musicologists to seek interpretation beyond “pure” research, warning “if musicologists intent on their researches leave undone the interpretative part of their work, they must not be surprised when others less fully equipped attempt it.”[39] Believing the discipline to be in crisis about writing about music for the public, Lang does not completely decry archival scholarship, but finds “there is no question that more vitality is called for,” imploring others to follow his literary and stylistic sensibilities to charm general audiences “into that blissful state.”[40] Lang’s focus is echoed in H. Wiley Hitchcock’s Grove Music Online entry for “Musicology in the United States,” where he concludes musicology’s greatest contemporary challenge remains “to make its impact felt outside its own domains,” especially within the general public, amongst performers, and figures in the music industry.[41]

Theory in Criticism: Taruskin and Gossett

When historically informed performance (HIP) claimed to “authentically” revive the past and became popular amongst classical audiences in the late-twentieth century, renowned musicologists Richard Taruskin and Philip Gossett critiqued the practice. From their differing analyses, out came two nuanced approaches to musicological interpretation that continue to reverberate in musicology today. Taruskin’s Text and Act found HIP’s popularity to be the result of novelty, commercial viability and a marketing of authenticity.[42] To him, HIP followed twentieth-century aesthetics, prioritized “textual authority from scores,” but did not possess authority or connect to traditions because it “masked itself with historical imperative”; real tradition in musicology came from connection to Charles Seeger’s notion of an “oral tradition.”[43] “Ultimate authority,” Taruskin claims, “rests not with the composer, not with the notation, but with Papa Doc, the musicologist.”[44] Seeing musicology as part of an oral tradition is Taruskin’s invitation for other musicologists to affirm their interpretive powers with regard to scores.

Gossett’s “Scholars and Performers” takes umbrage to the false dichotomy between academics and musicians as thinkers and doers and finds authenticity to be devoid of meaning, specifically with HIP’s supposed disciplinary mandate for strict score adherence.[45] Gossett observed that “how we listen today is inextricably related to how we live today.”[46] With that, he calls for ameliorative thinking around scholars and performers on the basis that both make negotiations in the process of moving from score to performance. Gossett’s conviction—an embrace of the contemporary—involved making decisions that call for, like Taruskin, decision making based on interpretation.

McClary’s Interdisciplinarity in “New” Musicology

Susan McClary’s “Constructions of Subjectivity in Schubert’s Music” is representative of the “New Musicology,” a 1990s disciplinary intervention that directly influenced public musicology because of its bold challenge of traditional academic theory; her work includes political and cultural dimensions into musical interpretation, thus making musicology more relevant and accessible to non-specialists.[47] Of the several insights McClary makes about music, she affirms a gendered performance of decisions, rather than an essentialist argument of Schubert’s sexuality signified in his works.[48] Her methods, including semiotics, narrativity, and cultural history, were “new” insofar as they rejected a purely musical analysis of elements such as harmony and rhythm, and invited perspectives of social body, politics, culture and meaning into musicology.[49] By invoking the scholarship of gender theorist Eve Kosofsky Sedgwick, for example, McClary provoked a democratization of musical meaning that broadened musicology’s appeal; her analysis incorporated culture and narrative so that non-specialists could also engage in meaningful interpretation. Not only did New Musicology politicized the canon, but it also called for a reappraisal of works dismissed by others based on its methods, thus fostering additional space for public discussion. This connection between musical elements and theories of gender and sexuality, for example, is a core tenet of public musicology.

Titon’s Terminology & Applied Ethnomusicology

The call for intention in Gordon’s response to Polzonetti recalls the interventions Jeff Todd Titon made toward an active, socially responsible sense of agency in public musicology, or as he would have it, “ethnomusicology in the public interest.”[50] His work features an emphasis on pragmatic action for non-specialist community empowerment that may even forego waiting to act before results are known.[51] Nonetheless, his vision of a reflexive theorization situating applied ethnomusicology thought in both worlds, academic and outside, is precisely a stance assumed by public musicologists.

Notably, he argues a broad conception of applied ethnomusicology, with public and public sector sub-disciplines inhering in the central goal of beneficial action in the public arena.[52] His distinctions see applied ethnomusicologists and public musicologists alike participating in a practice of enacting cultural policy interventions, advocacy and education, but views applied ethnomusicology as the covering term to others since the 1990s.[53] What some would call public sector ethnomusicology should simply be public ethnomusicology to Titon because it reflects a worthy, core purpose that centers intervention and social responsibility in the public arena.[54]

Public Musicology, 1939

As a contribution to the AMS Musicology Now blog, Carol Hess’s exploration of the 1939 AMS meeting is rich with insight concerning perennial disciplinary questions, as well as historic academic and public reception to the field.[55] Though journalists seemed to “find musicology compelling enough,” trailblazers such as Charles Seeger faced tepid reception in academia earlier in the century.[56] Remarking on the meeting’s ecumenical vision, its imperative from then-president Carleton Sprague Smith that musicologists should “impart [their findings] to others,” and the exposure of general and scholarly audiences to unfamiliar music, Hess all but identifies the meeting as a watershed for musicology and public musicology—which, for her, isn’t very different because they share perennial questions. These will be examined within the next section, which is dedicated to surveying contemporary public musicology. Aside from celebrating 1939, Hess succinctly defines musicology reflecting much of what has been theorized: “a living and breathing enterprise, a dynamic line of inquiry that embraces past, present, and future while enlightening the non-specialist public.”[57]

Recent Developments in Public Musicology

Recent scholarship reflects new and multiple applications, a strong turn towards the reparative in stance and practice and disciplinary calls for clear definition; represented here by Dromey, Cheng, and Henry, musicologists place a premium on knowledge exchange, public writing and non-specialist community engagement.

Introducing The Routledge Companion for Applied Musicology, Dromey accords with leading and contemporary scholars afield, using an applied-musicological lens to “recognize how musical expertise is (or could be) brought to bear in any number of domains or situations.”[58] He muses a myriad of opportunities for applied musicology and its new “branches” including public musicology, activities that leave “in their wake a diverse and fast-changing field whose scholarship and activities tend to take place in isolation from each other.”[59] Forensic musicology expert Peter Oxendale, for one, engages in public-facing work that invites attention from the popular press.[60] Despite disciplinary insularity, the attempt to blend scholarship with applied practice is evident in Robert D. Pearson’s use of Donald Francis Tovey’s program notes as overlooked scholarship; given its structural dissonance with current ideas of public music theory artifacts, Pearson answers McClary’s disciplinary call to re-evaluate works, composers, and in this case, practical applications as alternative but worthy sources of knowledge production.[61]

Reparative musicology resonates with the discipline theoretically and pragmatically. Expanding upon Suzanne Cusick and Eve Sedgwick’s notions of reparative rather than paranoid readings, Will Cheng’s Just Vibrations speaks to central ethical questions for the humanities from a musicological perspective and suggesting a turn from critical competition to communal care. Challenging notions of doing well—including adversarial academia—Cheng advocates doing good, enacting care and accessibility in the humanities, meaning “reaching back, lending help to those in need and seeking opportunities for care and repair.”[62] (emphasis mine) To him, the cacophony of musicology resembles the Tower of Babel, which can only be overcome “with learning to get along not despite but precisely because of human biodiversity and its ethical possibilities.”[63] His emphasis on love and beauty as a motivating impulse mandates better relations concerning the job market, as well as with the public.

Jasmine A. Henry’s critique of public musicology’s foundations advances reparative methodologies for new, equitable, and community-centered practices. Drawing from critical race theory, and theoretical frameworks at home in public musicology, praxis and communities as intellectual spaces, Henry employs Community-Based Qualitative Research (CBQR) to share authority between specialist and non-specialists; given its use of “an interpretive framework that recognizes multiple perspectives and values experiential knowledge,” directly challenging positivism.[64] Further, her acknowledgement of legitimate embodied knowledge of the marginalized, also known as counter-storytelling, provides perspectival richness to musicological analysis.[65] Both strategies employ reparative musicology in answering the question, “Who hears here?” to quote Guthrie Ramsey.

Concluding Definitions & Future Implications

Leah Broad curates relevant scholarship that considers what public musicology is, its rationale as a worthy endeavor and its capacity to support public engagement. My rendering of today’s discipline and tomorrow’s implications are greatly benefitted by her work. Though focused on Western classical music in the UK, Broad affirms public musicology as the agent of “real change” in contemporary musical life[66] with many definitions abounding afield.[67] Several are one-directional, but germane, including the act of “translating scholarship for non-academic audiences,”[68] and the promotion of results of recent research and discovery in the field of musicology.[69] Advocating instead the inclusion of “non-university organizations,” specialist adaptivity, change and flexibility, and reciprocity, she celebrates Naomi André’s engaged musicology[70] as one of several modes that seek to “democratize knowledge exchange” in collaboration with non-specialists.[71]

Reconciling top-down definitions with engaged musicology, Broad explores a theoretical midpoint where both musicological dissemination beyond academia and “reflexivity, reflection, and care from academics” occur.[72] There, Broad finds Dromey definition[73] approach, but not quite incorporate public history. Further, she suggests appropriate tone in public writing, which she finds challenging to define, but rests on a notion of familiarity to target audiences.[74] These components allow Broad to take the discipline into account in her own definition, “involvement conducting and disseminating musical research in a way that aims to be relevant, accessible, and understandable to a target audience outside of academia.”[75] Broads call for public engagement notably includes creating more professorships, an institutionalization that reminds of Spitta’s anxieties during musicology’s fledgling era.

Broad’s calls for public musicology are strikingly similar to Wissner’s in that her definition of “what a musicologist can be and do, who research is for, how it might be conducted, and the kind of research questions that can be asked” also considers structure, intention, method, and scope; in particular, her consideration for outputs in a variety of formats speaks directly to my own intervention for public musicological discourse. When Broad suggests that public musicological work may appear on YouTube, TikTok, Twitter/x, blog posts, newspaper articles, or podcasts, she democratizes the field for both specialists and audiences looking to exchange knowledge and produce community engagement. To her list, I add online spaces such as Reddit, Substack, and SoundCloud as forums where public musicologists can engage audiences alongside peer-reviewed journal articles, liner and program notes. Further, given the distance between music criticism and public musicology, I invite musicologists to continue working with instead of in observance of musicians and performers as interlocutors; the journalistic impulse, though part of the media landscape, could serve to be further balanced by endeavors attended by specialists and non-specialists.
