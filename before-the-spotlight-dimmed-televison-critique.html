Before the Spotlight Dimmed:

Dionne Warwick & Burt Bacharach at The Kraft Music Hall (1970)

Television Critique

Most marriages make the mistake in believing it takes two to make a thing go right. But it just so happens that three will give you better chances at success in matrimonial peace and order. Like most business arrangements, marriages fare better when opportunities for two-sided antagonism and opposition are minimized.

Like the holy trinity, triads foment balance and stability, and command respect and reverence. Though I can thank other alliances that flourished in stable longevity, such as Peter, Paul, and Mary or Janet Jackson, Jimmy Jam and Terry Lewis, nowhere is this more evident than in the musical marriage between composer Burt Bacharach, songwriter Hal David, and the divine Dionne Warwick.

Within pop music, where musical marriages seem to begin in heaven and quickly end in hellish flames, Bacharach, David, and Warwick prospered for nearly ten solid years—an eternity in the music industry. Between the three of them, an entire era (1962-1971) of successful releases for Scepter Records made for a unique soundtrack—a constant, alternative mélange of sound for the most turbulent decade in twentieth century America.

As a training music historian, I’ve learned how knowing the history and context about the process and creation of pop music can avail me to witness the magic of working relationships, such as the one between Warwick, David and Bacharach. I am left with the license to lose myself in the old magic of an enchanting voice, guided by poignant and fitting lyrics and propelled by pleasantly complex arrangements.

That said, I have minimal interest in serial television and film. I recognize that this retrospective examines a televised special from 1970, a time and sound that should seem foreign to me. (I’m a millennial.) I also recognize I lack the minimum of television acumen. When asked to review television, I became hard pressed to find a show to watch. For someone who devotes all TV time to MSNBC, Judge Judy reruns, and archival footage of female vocalists, however, The Kraft Music Hall program spoke to me early one morning in September. Roused from a deep sleep at 4 AM, I was startled but immediately connected to what I watched: Dionne Warwick singing, “Make It Easy on Yourself.” I took the cue and decided to review the program.

In June 1970, shortly after the release of I’ll Never Fall in Love Again, Warwick appeared with Bacharach on NBC as part of The Kraft Music Hall: An Evening with Burt Bacharach, one of a series of musical variety television specials that appeared on the network between 1958 and 1971. (Named after the sponsor, the Kraft corporation—the one and the same responsible for inedible mac and cheese.) Appearances from French singer and guitarist Sacha Distel and actor Joel Grey were broadcasted alongside Warwick’s segments; these scenes—perhaps scripted—provide us with an intimate look at the moments that reify the relationship Bacharach enjoyed with David and Warwick.

Dressed in a show gown of purple chiffon with a green and pink foliate motif, Warwick beautifully performed, “What the World Needs Now,” a new arrangement of her 1962 demo “Make It Easy on Yourself,” as well as “Loneliness Remembers What Happiness Forgets,” the last of which also featured in a warm recording session between vocalist and her composer. Led by Bacharach on piano, the two reminisced on their years together while performing a medley of earlier hits including “Walk on By,” “Alfie,” and “I’ll Never Fall in Love Again” amongst others.

I admire Warwick’s vocal timing and enjoy her comedic restraint in her subtle retorts to Bachrach’s suggestions about songs, vocal range, and their partnership—funny, but likely toned-down for television. For example, when Bacharach mentioned paying Warwick $15 per demo, she quips, “Nuh-uh. It was twelve dollars and fifty cents.” The laughs continue when she’s asked if she remembers her demo for “Make it Easy on Yourself.” Perhaps to throw minimal shade, Warwick pretends to forget songs she recorded for $12.50. The chemistry full of warm wit, Warwick’s stardom finds parity in partnership with David and Bacharach.

Together, the company of three created a pop holy trinity. The unseen songwriter in David, breathes the words of life into Bacharach’s arrangements, which then find precision and accuracy as they leave Warwick’s lips. Peppered in attractive charisma, Bacharach, the musically omnipotent composer, resembles the reassuring father who encourages his child to sing songs requiring two octaves. And of course, the public-facing counterpart, Warwick basks in the glow of the spotlight. She glorifies, converts, and saves all through the gospel of music. What Bacharach calls her “perfection” is her smart and subtle sense of lilt, which is on full display in the special. For those to care to hear, she shares her divinity to save us all from perdition.

Luck would have it, Bacharach reflects, that working with professionals like Dionne Warwick and Hal David would result in the several hit singles, of which, no such formula existed. And while I agree that the formula did not exist in the individual songs written and performed, the magic in the marriage between the trio is found in their foundation, “built on love and understanding. Her love of music and our understanding of her great talent” induced our enchantment with Warwick’s seeming ease for many years while she sang Bacharach’s complex arrangements and expressed David’s melancholic sentiment.

Yet, as luck would have it, I reflect, this televised special is bittersweet in retrospect. Soon after its airing, the match made in heaven—a critical component of Warwick’s new recording contract with Warner Bros.—would divorce. This time, over the monetary failure caused by the box office bomb, Lost Horizon (1973). Warwick’s career would not recover until her comeback in 1979’s Dionne, but this time, without David and Bacharach. What I witnessed at The Kraft Music Hall occurred before the spotlight dimmed. We were entreated to a final moment in a marriage of sweet musical magic, the likes of which, are a beloved rarity today.

Bridging music, media, and public criticism.
