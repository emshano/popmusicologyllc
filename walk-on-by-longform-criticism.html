Walk On By: Dionne Warwick and the Craft of Longform Criticism

RESEARCH DOCUMENT Open original · Google Drive

Kwame K. Ocran

Second Draft

Dionne Warwick: An Introduction

It is striking that the voice that belongs to Dionne Warwick, ever changing, ever wizening, has seldom been presented as a singular entity. From the outset of her storied career, with 1962’s “Don’t Make Me Over,” Warwick dominated American airwaves as a solo artist that simultaneously worked as the public-facing third of a partnership between herself, and her writing partners, composer Burt Bacharach and lyricist Hal David. Like many of those that followed, that first single, a triumph of black female vocality, is notable for its lyrics on love, her intended holding so much power over her. Though she sings of love with resignation, she begs him, in heightened passion, to accept her for who she is. Warwick’s performance, the fiery determination to retain her authentic interiority, is first among the several reasons “Don’t Make Me Over” is regarded as a feminist classic. Later, inevitably, that voice would grow older into its interpretative gifts. but this time, assisted by the genius of Clive Davis, whose expertise and knowledge of finding the right material for Dionne’s voice, the result was a strong renaissance for her, beginning in 1979 and catching the attention of Warwick’s long-considered rival, Aretha Franklin.

At times, Warwick re-entered public consciousness with regards to matters other than singing. There appeared Dionne, the public face of the Psychic Friends hotline, leading the charge against hip-hop, castigating Tupac and Snoop Dogg, drawing the ire of Method Man, and eventually becoming the Queen of Twitter with her circumspect auntie vibe. The occasion of this paper celebrates these hallmarks in all their awesomeness, their glamour, and even their zaniness. In. the midst of the cultural cachet Warwick had amassed in these ventures, her music has become somewhat obscured by the other aspects of her varied career. However, first and foremost, Dionne Warwick is an artist. “Walk On By” intends to relive significant moments of Warwick’s career with an ear to invite those who enjoy her to return once again to her music. The goal is to remind listeners of Warwick’s extraordinary vocal gifts, the reason why anybody ever cared about her in the first place.

Warwick’s storied career noted, it is equally striking that critical evaluations of her voice and oeuvre fall short of recognizing her tremendous talent. Warwick’s voice, a commanding and lithe contralto, performed a new interiority for the black female: one of sophisticated, arresting restraint. Though redolent of an aggrieved, serious young lover in song, perhaps Warwick’s ditziness offstage appeared to not concord with the air of genius or of serious musicianship. More than likely, it should be noted that the critical perspective onto popular music placed hyper focus onto white rock and rollers primarily. If a black woman found critical success in the appraisal of her work, she was likely Aretha Franklin, or a traditional soul singer who, through the rawness of her vocalizations, provided a voyeuristic view into her interiority, namely through the performance of black pain. Through Warwick’s markedly restrained vocalization, /David’s lyrics, and Bacharach’s arrangements, Warwick’s singles would continue to crossover from soul into pop, a complication that made it too easy for less discerning critics to regard her merely as a pop singer.

Dionne Warwick, born Marie Dionne Warrick on December 12th, 1940, came of age enveloped by the strong currents of the black church. Its foundation reverberated in the religious and economic lives of her family. Her parents, Lee Drinkard and Mansel Warrick, both worked behind the scenes within the gospel music industry—her father, a gospel record promoter for Chess Records, her mother, manager and sometimes performer with the family-membered Drinkard Singers. This would have significant implications for decisions Warwick made throughout her career, as well as within her highly guarded personal life. Rooted in the faith-based tradition, Warwick’s grandfather, a minister, figured prominently in her proximity to Christian values and music. Singing gospel in her early years, Warwick formed a trio called the Gospelaires, alongside her sister Dee Dee and her aunt Cissy Drinkard. Performing first at the New Hope Baptist Church choir in Newark, the Gospelaires sang in local churches and on college campuses, as well as in theaters. The three performed and won Amateur Night at the Apollo in 1957. Later, after stardom, Warwick would reflect on her artistry and the affirmation of her faith, and the attribute her success to the Holy Spirit as her foundation.

Dionne Warwick and Stardom

In 1959, she commenced her other work; Warwick pursued music academically—achieving a master’s degree at the Hartt School of Music at the University of Hartford, which meant, according to Dr. Lonnie Bunch, the director the Smithsonian Museum of African American History, that “Warwick put in work to achieve her goals, including the obtaining both Bachelor’s and Master’s degrees, and her ability to read, write, and sight-read music.” Bunch’s point has several implications for how Warwick should be studied. Given her desire to work with and engage in music, Warwick pragmatically pursued music education, should popular performance not pan out. And when it did, Warwick signaled genius in parleying her musical education into her career as a vocalist; Warwick was later able to successfully interpret Bacharach’s difficult arrangements, due to her ability to sight-read. This not only granted her footing as David and Bacharach’s primary interpreter, but it also cemented her agency as someone who decided the timbre of her material.

By 1960, Warwick and her elder sister, Dee Dee, formed the Sweet Inspirations along with the remaining members of her mother’s Drinkard Singers. The group enjoyed enormous success as session and background vocalists. It was at one of these recording sessions in which the Gospelaires sang for the Drifters in 1961 when Bacharach was struck by Warwick’s voice and presence. He requested she record demos for him and Hal David. Thus, the marriage of sorts, began to take place. When Scepter Records, a New York record company heard her voice, they agreed to sign her. In a record contract negotiated by Bacharach & David, Warwick’s father (who parleyed his own expertise from his Chess Records days), and Scepter Records, Warwick would not be signed as a solo artist, but in fact, as part of the songwriting team of Bacharach & David. This unique arrangement between artist, songwriter, and record company meant that Warwick would perform singles that her writers produced. During her tenure at Scepter (1961- 1971), Warwick saw David as a poet, given his exceptional abilities as a lyricist. Bacharach, too, must have appeared as a virtuoso arranger for Warwick’s vocal talents. The result produced what was heard as revolutionary music which sparked confusion over racialized genres and Warwick’s classification.

During her early stardom, Dionne Warwick was described as “very tough, very cool” and self-possessed; irrepressible, some might have said. In a run-in in the segregated South while touring with Sam Cooke, Warwick’s defiance and willingness to take chances against a prejudiced waitress put her feistiness on full display. Admired as a statuesque, powerful-voiced pop and rhythm and blues singer who had formal music training in addition to a gospel church upbringing, there were too many genres to associate with Warwick’s unique sound. Comparing herself to Ella Fitzgerald and Lena Horne, Warwick had been after the same ubiquity, to maintain the utmost artistic integrity and identity. Known for her unique styling as well as her strong appeal to young as well as older audiences, both white and black, Warwick crossed over from rhythm and blues into the popular genre without alienating either audience. Her success as a crossover artist calls into question her genre categorization; from her vocalizations and intonations alone, she could be categorized as 1960s pop, gospel, rhythm-and-blues, or jazz. The realization that Warwick borrows from but does not neatly fit into any of these traditions makes a good case for the abolishment of genre altogether. In a 1966 profile on her origins, Warwick quips that it is up to the appraiser, not the artist to assign genre, insisting to writer Lorraine Alterman, “You figure it out.” Her flair at deflection makes the challenge of relating her music and interiority to a particular genre increasingly difficult, because it seemed that young Warwick would not help critics make sense of her or her music. What made sense was Warwick’s immediate appeal, demonstrating her place in every market and demographic by remaining true to her foundation: her faith. The same profile would see her reject the idea of recording “white soul or hard rock songs” because she sang from “the gospel tradition.”

As much as young Dionne Warwick was self-effacing about popular music classification, she was even more introspective concerning the affairs of her private life, celebrity, and the implications of her music. In a 1967 interview, she seemed to be adamant about maintaining a semblance of quietude and normalcy away from the adulation of celebrity. She also claims a sense of responsibility for the messages she shares in her music, taking the moral high ground against performing messages “other than something that’s true or something that has a good foundation for saying.” Her conviction has proven true throughout her six-decade career, where the quality of her music can generally be assessed around themes of friendship, love, heartbreak, and reunion. If an aesthetic evaluation can be drawn based on the positionality that Warwick inhabits, her work is performed from an interiority that allows her to deliver powerful, emotive lyrics over difficult arrangements on the strength of her technical abilities as a musician and her virtuosic interpretative flourishes. Further, Warwick’s beauty and sound have been the object of obsession for many who found the songstress hard to place. Some appraised Warwick to be unclassifiable by genre, but more than capable of commanding several popular forms. Glowing introspective pieces detailing Warwick’s attributes and virtues noted her sense of diplomacy over the comparisons she drew to contemporary Diana Ross, for example. Contemporary music journalists seemed to see a keen focus in Warwick continuing to work with trusted collaborators to make music she found beautiful. Her extremely honest expression and performance motivated one such journalist to defend her vehemently from those who dismissed her as artificial because she did not pen her songs.

After her first single, “Don’t Make Me Over” at Scepter Records, Dionne Warwick would release eighteen albums in ten years, notching an unprecedented twenty-two Top 40 hits on the Billboard Hot 100 Chart. By the time she parted ways with Scepter in 1971 after Very Dionne (1970), Warwick had become an international superstar based on her viability as a recording artist, her reputation as a superior vocalist, and the magical trifecta she formed with Hal David & Burt Bacharach. In addition to her solo work, Warwick would occasionally stand in with the Sweet Inspirations and the Shirelles throughout the 1960s, most notably appearing on the 1961 classic “Will You Love Me Tomorrow,” a standard she later remade with the remaining Shirelles on her live album, Hot! Live and Otherwise (1981).

Warwick’s initial period of international renown had been somewhat hard-fought. For example, British vocalist Cilla Black’s recoding of Warwick’s single, “Anyone Who Had a Heart” in 1964 fared better on the charts, much to Warwick’s disappointment. Further, her first European release featured a white woman on its cover, causing quite a stir when it became apparent that Warwick was black. Performances at the Olympia Theater in Paris and as the star of the 1965 Cannes Television and Film Festival were heralded on the heels of “Walk on By,” which reached the top ten of the UK charts “in an era of chart domination by such groups as the Beatles, Stones, Searchers, and others.”

While in Europe, particularly in Paris, Warwick was taken under the wing of Hollywood actress Marlene Dietrich during her first tour. It was Dietrich who proved instrumental in introducing Warwick to European fashion couture, thus contributing a significant change to Dionne’s sartorial sensibilities. There remains the question. given what we know of Dietrich—of her bisexuality and her predilection for the underground bars of Paris and Europe—and of Dionne Warwick—a young Black American ingenue raised within the strict confines of the black church—of whether more can be speculated of their relationship than a simple mentorship and sartorial advice. Did Dietrich introduce Warwick to the thriving gay bars of Europe? Could they have become lovers? Quite possibly, but given the lack of evidence and Warwick’s desire to guard private matters, it remains a matter of speculation.

More can be learned about Warwick from her ill-fated marriage to William Elliot in 1966. The union unceremoniously ended as soon as it began by Mexican divorce, some weeks after the “I do’s”, only to begin again the following year and finally end for good in 1975. The marriage produced two sons, Damon and David, whom Warwick largely raised as a single mother. Not much else is discussed about the marriage within most of the resources consulted for this piece. And while William Elliot is no longer with us to consult on the matter, more must have occurred between the two to pronounce the end of the marriage (twice). Whatever brought the end about, it must have been large enough for Warwick to contravene the dictums set forth about the indelibility of marriage. Warwick’s strong Christian beliefs must have been rocked to their foundation.

Much praise and adulation came from Warwick’s near peerless position as a live performer and the strength of her musical releases during her tenure at Scepter. Upon writing a review of Warwick’s vocalization at The Regal Theater in Chicago in April 1964, writer Guy Stevens remarked that she “managed to create virtually the same sound as she gets on her records, something we’re just not used to in the United Kingdom.” Others have observed that Warwick’s meteoric rise to critical and commercial acclaim were the result of her singing, with descriptions of her as an “extremely gifted artist” with a unique song styling. Album reviews were encouraging. Lorraine Alterman highlights as beautiful then-future Dionne Warwick classics such as “Alife,” a rendition of Bob Dylan’s “Blowin’ in the Wind,” and “Something There to Remind Me” in her back-to-back reviews on December 1966’s Here Where There Is Love, and September 1967’s The Windows of the World.

Warwick’s stature grew to a fever pitch in April 1970 with her widely reviewed performance at Royal Albert Hall, where Alan Smith for the New Musical Express discusses Warwick’s dazzling performance, notably involving the distinct characterization of her voice as having the quality of “smoke and coffee ice cream.” Royston Eldridge’s review of Warwick heralded her as a rare talent capable of overcoming any sort of categorization in music; Warwick is rather a mélange of several musical forms “combined with an ability to communicate an emotion to all kinds of people…an emotion that’s best expressed through the love songs of Bacharach and David.” Warwick’s styling became associated with words such as sensitivity, church, and musical, as others became excited at its enchanting quality; Dave Godin for Blues and Soul provided a sumptuous analysis of Warwick’s singing, praising how “every nuance and inflection was there, and indeed when she sang, she showed that there are few who can equal her in any way—she was faultless.”

Not only is Warwick celebrated by Godin, but he also comes to her aid in reference to critics who review music on the vanguard; comparing Warwick to chart competitor Diana Ross, Godin identifies the latter as the “white man’s image of black womanhood,” which interests my study of Warwick and her reception over time through the volitions of the white press and the critical elite. Granted, Warwick’s critical response, despite the numerous examples demonstrating the contrary, is tepid at best. Prominent music critic Robert Christgau had not assigned any of Warwick’s albums with an ‘A’ until her All Time Greatest Hits Collection arrived in 1989. Several albums from her 1970s output had been scored within the ‘C’ range—even similarly named but stylistically different Dionne (1972) and Dionne (1979) had both been awarded a “C+.” Robert Hilburn of the Los Angeles Times had nothing to say of Warwick’s career. Pioneering female music critic Lillian Roxon interviewed Warwick in 1972, finding the singer to occupy a simultaneity in being self-possessed, aristocratic and glacial, while also having the playfulness of a little girl. Perhaps Roxon’s feminist reading of Warwick’s manifold personalities could be revisited as prescient. By glacial and aristocratic, Roxon may have been hinting at Warwick’s physical and psychic distance—a distance, no less, self-devised—between celebrity and personal lives. A performing style that centers a distinct, regal black female vocality, Warwick recorded her voice to be close enough to the listener’s ear to arrest it with her warm and lithe singing, but never close enough to be arrested by genre or categorization. Warwick was identified by music journalists as a gifted performer; few, like Roxon, went as far as to admire the nuance and complication of Dionne Warwick the person.

Dionne Warwick and the 1970s

As fortunes in American popular music go, the lush and masterful blend of pop, R&B, and show tunes that made Warwick famous went out of vogue in the early 1970s. What’s more is that her beloved collaborators, David & Bacharach would dissolve their partnership. Bacharach demanded higher profit shares in the future royalties of the music he produced with David in the 1973 remake, Lost Horizon. Unfortunately, not only was the film-musical a box-office flop, but it also irrevocably strained the successful partnership between David and Bacharach to the extent that the two would not work together on future Dionne Warwick projects. Because the terms of her recording contract resembled the one signed at Scepter Records the decade prior, one which required the involvement of the Bacharach-David production team, the dissolution of her production team would jeopardize Warwick’s place at Warner Brothers Records, her new recording home. Ultimately, Lilian Roxon’s deep reflection of Warwick would be that “these changes could not have been easy. She is uncomfortable with change.” Again, Roxon may well have been prophetic, Warwick embraced unprecedented coming changes: signing with Warner Brothers Records in 1971, ending her partnership with Bacharach and David. Writing for Melody Maker on the anticipation of her first album with Warner Bros., Dionne (1972), Michael Watts made note of an added ‘e’ at the end of Warwick’s last name (rendering it as Warwicke), a suggestion the singer took from a numerologist who incorrectly predicted increased fortunes and prosperity. At the start of her tenure with Warner Brothers, Warwick was not a black soul artist, neither was she exceptionally politically conscious, nor had she any agenda to push. Simply, she advocated for herself as a universally loved crossover artist and expressed her aspirations for Hollywood and Broadway (after all, 1972 was the year Diana Ross starred in the Billie Holiday biopic, Lady Sings the Blues).

However, instead of immense success, Warwick endured several hitless years, producing eight studio albums that managed only one number one hit: 1974’s duet with The Spinners, “Then Came You,” under the production guidance of Thom Bell, the architect of the Philadelphia Sound. The 1970s would see Warwick refuse to record disco; as she would later reflect in her 2022 documentary, Don’t Make Me Over, she did not find Disco to be “[her] thing.” David Nathan lamented Warwicke’s downturned fortunes in commercial and critical success during this period, speculating that the trite recording material at Warner Bros. came from Warwick losing the “soul feeling” in her artistry. The critique is ironic. Nathan, a Warwick fan, suggests she “do an Aretha,” as in render her career similarly to Franklin’s, when it turned out to be Aretha who would “do a Dionne” with Clive Davis. Nathan also poses serious questions about the career trajectories of R&B artists, wondering, for instance, that despite there being no doubt in Warwick establishing herself as a formidable charting artist, there may be a possibility that she sacrificed her “soul” at the same time.

Upon the release of Warwick’s just Being Myself (1973), largely penned by former Motown writers Holland-Dozier-Holland, Nathan writes for Blues & Soul, lambasting the press corps for the questions they asked of the singer, including “what makes a female feminine?” An interesting question to pose to Warwick; the brunt of praise she received for her appearance was due to her high cheekbones, slender figure, and sartorial sophistication. In thinking through her struggles and upheavals during her time at Warner Brothers, Nathan published Warwick’s candid dissatisfaction with the album’s results. Nathan ends his review undeterred that Warwick would ultimately give “other contemporary soul female artists like Aretha, Diana, and Shirley Bassey a run for their money.” Still, the culminating consensus is that save for “Then Came You,” Warwick’s tenure at Warner Bros. had proved quite difficult for the singer. It is also worth mentioning that tastes, genre, and formats had begun to change as Warwick left Scepter for Warner Bros. Record companies became keen on delivering music that audiences would consume, and much of Warwick’s post-Scepter output did not deliver the goods.

In the late seventies, at the nadir of her career, Dionne Warwick would meet during a shared appearance on The Dinah Shore Show, the record executive and music mogul Clive Davis. They discussed Dionne’s possible exit from the music industry. Davis implored Warwick to stay, intimating that despite her desire to leave the music industry, he intended to show her that the music industry had not abandoned her. Davis recruited and signed Warwick to Arista Records and teamed her with singer-songwriter and producer Barry Manilow. Davis commissioned Manilow to compose a song for Dionne. The result, “I’ll Never Love This Way Again,” would become Warwick’s comeback song. Other songs were commissioned, such as a re-recording of “After You” from Cissy Drinkard Houston’s solo material, and the Issac Hayes produced, “Déjà vu.”

The next record, her first for Arista, Dionne (1979) resurrected Warwick’s profile from oblivion. Hugely successful, the album became Warwick’s first platinum record, with two Top 20 singles. Her comeback was established and manifested in the record-executive helmed project combined with the talent of a vocalist equipped with cultural cachet. That was the model Davis established with Warwick in 1979; the 1980s would see several revivals of soul singers, including Patti Labelle, and Warwick’s rival, Aretha Franklin. The executive-guided selection and curation of recording material figures heavily into the start of careers for the likes of Mariah Carey, Whitney Houston, and Celine Dion. Furthermore, the album returned the diminishing fortunes of Warwick’s career itself. Not only did she find renewed commercial success, at the 1980 Grammy Awards Ceremony, she received two awards for Best Pop Vocal Performance, Female and Best R&B Vocal Performance, Female, for “I’ll Never Love This Way Again” and “Déjà vu,” becoming the first African American woman to win the award in both categories on the same night.

After the Dionne era, Warwick would continue to regularly place songs on Billboard’s Adult Contemporary Chart, and achieve popular hits, such as 1980’s “No Night So Long,” 1982’s “Heartbreaker” and an R&B Top 10 with “Love Power,” a quiet storm duet with singer Jeffrey Osbourne. Recalling the process of “Heartbreaker,” Warwick recalls being “badgered” into recording the song, despite its being penned by Barry Gibb of the Bee Gees. The song would prove to be her biggest European hit, and would also be a smash on several formats: #10 on the main Billboard Hot 100 Chart, #14 on the Billboard Hot R&B Chart, and her eighth #1 Hot Adult Contemporary Singles Chart.

Warwick: The Second Act

During her return to prominence, Warwick began to leverage her talents to contribute to philanthropic and social causes. In 1985, she marshaled the vocal and instrumental talents of Stevie Wonder, Gladys Knight, and Elton John to record what would be the song of her career. “That’s What Friends Are For” would see her reunite with Burt Bacharach. The single went to #1 on the Billboard Hot 100 and earned Warwick’s fifth Grammy award for Best Pop Vocal Performance, Duo or Group. She made the decision to donate the profits the song generated to amfAR, the American Foundation for AIDS Research—an amount far exceeding $10 million. Nevertheless, modern analysis of Warwick’s days as spokesperson have cast the singer into a grimier spotlight, mentioning that the brunt of charity donations had been eaten by administrative costs for lavish travel and meals for herself and her associates.

The cause for AIDS Research seemed to be an odd fit for someone of Warwick’s religious sensibilities. Even though the onset of the AIDS crisis primarily affected gay men, ironically it was Warwick’s adherence to her faith that motivated her desire to be of service. The eighties would see Warwick fundraise for philanthropic research, taking a visible, vocal stance on the crisis when many celebrities were afraid to come forward. Many, including Clive Davis, believed Warwick could have lived comfortably on the song’s royalties. And though that may have been true, Warwick’s decision to give the song’s royalties to amfAR ironically evolved her public celebrity and changed the trajectory of AIDS in America. The song became the anthem of the AIDS movement, and its success led Warwick to be made a health ambassador by President Ronald Regan in 1988.

Warwick also participated in the recording of the decade’s most famous charity musical event, USA for Africa’s “We Are the World” (1985) and happily donated her talents to the 1985 global concert, “Live Aid.” Further global recognition came in the form of gaining her star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame, becoming the first African American woman to appear in a command performance before Queen Elizabeth II, and singing with the National Opera Company of Japan.

With a desire to explore careers in film, television and Broadway, Warwick parleyed her stardom and influence into other arenas of popular culture from 1969 onward, giving a well-reviewed performance in the box office bomb Slaves (1969), hosting the pilot and several subsequent episodes of the Top 40 dance show, Solid Gold, and hosting several TV series and specials, including the 14th Annual Grammy Awards in 1972 and the First Annual Soul Train Music Awards in 1987. She even went as far as to host her own daytime talk show, Dionne and Friends for one season in 1990. Perhaps less dignified but certainly a widely viewed platform, Warwick aired a series of ubiquitous late-night infomercials for the Psychic Friends Network.

Despite reaching new career milestones, Warwick’s life was set back by several personal losses, including the deaths of her sister Dee Dee and cousins Whitney and Bobbi Krisitna Houston—all within a seven-year period. Warwick would also twice file Chapter 7 bankruptcy over gross financial mismanagement, as recent as 2013. Still performing, Warwick is known for creating a soulful sound that transcends race and genre, as well musical boundaries. She is also known for selling an excess of 100 million records and winning six Grammys in the course of a sixty-year career.

Dionne Warwick: Her Critical Evaluation

Aside from the supportive news media related to Dionne Warwick’s career and artistry, there are critical issues regarding Warwick’s aesthetic evaluation. Given the sheer amount of fanfare that critics have given her contemporaries Diana Ross and Aretha Franklin, Warwick has yet to achieve the same regard for her music, her vocal inflections, and her sheer longevity within the music industry. Warwick, is more than deserving of additional critical attention.

Coming to prominence first, Dionne Warwick scored major pop hits so that Aretha Franklin could cover them. Warwick, though talented, had a sophisticated sound that critics could not quickly classify; neither was she the type of black female singer that Franklin had been. The latter seemed to have been steeped in the church tradition where Warwick was thought to have merely dipped her toe. Of course, this is not the case. Warwick’s original version of “I Say a Little Prayer” is a quiet, cool rumination said to oneself before a church service, or during every free moment the narrator can use to secure the love of her intended through the contemplative power of prayer. Warwick withholds and practices restraint in the mediation of her thoughts. She embodies this stance finely with Bacharach’s arrangement and David’s lyrics. On the other hand, though enjoyable, Franklin’s raucous interpretation makes the private public. Where Warwick keeps prayer to herself, it is Franklin who declares her prayer at the bombastic heights of Praise & Worship, into the microphone, already involving the background singers of her intention as collection plates traverse the congregation. Side by side, the interpretative elements of both can exist for different moments in prayer, but the latter aims to outshout the stolid presence of the original in a way that somewhat misinterprets Bacharach and David’s intentions for the song. If we are to assume their intended sound was Dionne Warwick’s version, then the contemplative nature of prayer is the aim. Franklin’s willingness to declare love in her public prayers, though moving, misses the opportunity that the original captures so well—the trepidation that comes with the possibility of love, one that cannot be guaranteed by one’s own doing.

Warwick had been singing solo long before Ross could outshine her remaining Supremes. When juxtaposed, Dionne Warwick and Diana Ross, the latter commands a discography that instantly affects listeners with an arsenal of Motown-made, R&B-pop that perennially led to a high-level of commercial appeal—a fame that allowed Ross to do what Warwick only aspired to: star in Hollywood feature films. Ross’s acclaim as an actress may be the result of her early commercial success as a vocalist; Ross’s voice is a slight, high soprano that appealed most to white audiences in America and abroad. Ross, short and petite, possessed the persona and the film role (a drink and drug-fueled Billie Holiday) to garner a white audience to attend Lady Sings the Blues. Warwick, with her serious skills as an interpretative vocalist, would not see Ross as her competition. After all, Warwick had starred and engaged in an on-screen interracial romance with Stephen Boyd in Slaves (1969). And yet, Don’t Make Me Over features a scene with a young Warwick, standing upon the Brooklyn Bridge, making beautiful poses with a pastel-colored scarf. I am immediately transported to my recollection of Ross’s 1983 Central Park performance where she too glamorously held a bright orange scarf against the sway of wind and storm. Warwick’s earlier posing, subdued and more private, conjures deliberate parameters for seeing one in context with the other; whereas Warwick shielded her interiority, even in the most emotive of performances, Ross’s large eyes could hardly attempt to conceal her zeal for the people.

There remains Aretha Franklin, who, save for maybe Patti LaBelle, was perhaps the only vocal peer in Warwick’s class that could pose a serious challenge to her vocal prowess. Though Warwick and Franklin differed in their artistries and performativity, there are comparable moments in both of their careers: both were African American solo female artists that debuted in the 1960s (a time when Warwick proved to be the bigger star), both renewed their fortunes in commercial success after lulled periods of lukewarm reception in the 1970s. Both would release era-defining, colossally successful singles during their second prime in the 1980s under the guidance of Clive Davis. With all that Dionne Warwick has accomplished, why is it that critics have only recently begun to recognize the trails she blazed with her voice? About five years ago, I asked music critic Robert Christgau about his low estimation of other female vocalists. He responded, “Besides Aretha Franklin, I don’t like big voices.” Certainly, an intervention on Warwick’s behalf deserves expression.

In grounding Dionne Warwick in the music criticism of her career, I recognized that her oeuvre, from 1962’s “Don’t Make Me Over” through to her recent collaborations with her son Damon, and Dolly Parton, span the emergence of rock criticism and its succeeding generations. Warwick has outlived many of the writers we’ve read over the years, given her longevity in the entertainment and music industries. Her major charting years have seen the racial divisions that operated within music critical circles, which had been historically a corps of mostly white writers focusing their attentions on white rockers and raw black singers. Warwick’s longevity withstood their eventual changing of guard and now boasts a popular command over today’s multifaceted mediascape. Perhaps this work can begin to address the absence of careful criticism of her music.

If we investigate, from start to present, the contours of critical opinions of Dionne Warwick’s career, the principal cause for her sanction from the upper echelons of critical acclaim will reveal itself. Warwick, who enjoyed the deep probing analyses of two releases on the online music publication, Pitchfork, is particularly telling of the main criticism of her oeuvre. In his review-in-hindsight of her first album, Presenting Dionne Warwick (1963), writer Andy Cush references Warwick’s own 2010 memoir As I See It, where she found that as she crossed over to greater popular success in national television appearances and higher charting, “something that came as a big surprise: the decline of airplay for my records on African American radio.”1 She continues, “In fact, when I asked one of New York’s premier jocks, Rocky G…why he was not playing my records on his show, he told me I was ‘too white.’”

The “too white” complaint has long plagued Warwick’s earlier career, as did the creative output of Bacharach and David. It didn’t matter that as a black woman, Warwick would employ her smart inflections and nimble singing that attracted listening ears beyond race. The racial dynamics at play in the “too white” sentiment seem nearly too complicated to untangle, especially when the radical intricacy of Warwick’s music is concerned. Beyond Warwick’s vocality, there are other subtle ingredients that informed her sound, such as Bacharach’s love of jazz and bebop. Perhaps the mélange between the three revealed something of a universal element that most obviously set it apart from the more straightforward R&B records Rocky G would have been playing.

Though Warwick transcended racial lines, single after single, she may have adapted an image that portended pop sensibilities over R&B; I mean to say that as Warwick continued to appeal to pop audiences, and because of the racialized divisions created by genre, once an artist of Warwick’s stature crosses over, there is a fine line they must walk to keep their total fanbase happy. To say in 2023, that Dionne Warwick is not black enough to qualify as R&B would be silly. But part of the comedy of the situation is rooted in critical evaluations that seriously, and lazily, attempt to classify her as white-aspiring; Warwick proffers, in every manifestation of voice, an unquestionable black female authenticity. Sophistication transcends race, and is, along with the authentic, the foundation of Warwick’s sound and vocality. The following comments evaluate her voice and its transformation from the early 1960s until the early 2020s.

Dionne Warwick: the Vocalist and the Singer

Not one to be immune to scandal, Dionne Warwick was found to have had eleven “marijuana cigarettes” in her bag in May 2002. Though she claims to not partake in the drug, there is yet significant evidence of Warwick smoking cigarettes. Even in the promotional video for “I Say a Little Prayer,” she can be seen holding a cigarette nonchalantly in the Parisienne style. Granted, she came of age in an era where smoking was more socially accepted than at present. However, I present these facts to begin to explain the transformation, the deepening and widening of Warwick’s voice, which has been mediated by time, age, and extra-curricular activities.

As a vocalist, Warwick began her career with a light voice and versatility in range. That range would slowly shrink, as most voices do, but gain a silken quality and a masterful interpretative skill. In Warwick’s documentary, Burt Bacharach shared that she had a large vocal range and versatility, which can be heard on “Don’t Make Me Over” (1962) and “Anyone Who Had a Heart” (1964), both of which feature soft singing as high mezzo soprano as well as soul belting. Critic Hilton Als wrote in 2013 that earlier in Warwick’s career, her “range allowed her to sing contralto low notes and to soar as high as a soprano.” These assessments are apt.

Coupled with previous images of Dionne Warwick as a young woman, the simultaneous girlishness and glacial distance reveal a talented young artist who faced difficulty in sorting out her sound. Nevertheless, the warm Warwick sound was a sensation that would continue to make itself known in songs like “Alife” (1967) and “Déjà Vu” (1979) as her voice reached its matured sound in the late 1970s. In Don’t Make Me Over, the late Olivia Newton-John mentioned that Warwick had a melancholic tone. Stevie Wonder reflected on the capacity of Warwick’s voice to “allow people to see and look forward to the best part of themselves.” When Warwick debuted, her voice carried a sense of agility that manifested in lilt flourishes in songs such as the refrain in 1969’s “I’ll Never Fall in Love Again.”

Notably, between 1963 and 2023, as Warwick’s voice deepened, it adapted a softer delivery that speaks to Bacharach’s assessment of her voice. That said, I believe the performance of Dionne Warwick, the sophisticated madame on records such as “Déjà vu” could not have been accomplished with the same precision earlier than 1975; the fullness of her soft sound and delivery in lyrics such as, “This is insane, all you did was say hello” make her delirium believable. Even to this day, she remains the consummate performer, knowledgeable of her voice’s capability after six decades of performance, aware of the harmonization that best suits a live 2023 performance of “Walk On By,” to name just one example.

As a singer, however, Warwick wields her instrument with a sense of subtlety, restraint, and a sensitivity toward conveying emotion. And while she performs with emotive power connected to the music, her role as interpreter stops there. Here is where Warwick is the opaquest: on stage, she is not communicating or emoting from her own interiority, but instead from the interpretation that best suits the song in performance, an interpretation of the making of Warwick herself, together with input from Bacharach and David. Nevertheless, such secular performative sensibilities led Cissy Houston to remark in Don’t Make Me Over that although Warwick was raised in the church, she was not really gospel and had another “spirit” in her. Perhaps Houston is speaking to Warwick’s ineffable vocal presence; unclassifiable, Warwick’s artistry— specifically her tenures at Scepter and Warner Bros.—exists in a liminal space, just beyond the traditional sounds expected in music, and relegated by racialized genre, at the time. It was her choice to join Clive Davis at Arista Records that ultimately secured her Grammy wins in Pop and R&B in 1980. By then, she cultivated a dedicated, multi-genre fanbase that settled—once and for all—that question of race. Whatever genre Dionne Warwick is, she is beloved.

In response to the sudden assassination of the Rev. Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr. in April 1968, Dionne Warwick performed a heartfelt rendition of “Battle Hymn of the Republic” on The Ed Sullivan Show. Warwick’s memorable performance, a somber, moving tribute to the slain Civil Rights leader, demonstrated her awareness of pertinent issues as well as her commitment to use her voice and celebrity in the advancement of worthwhile causes.

Cosmic, Comic Zaniness: Contemporary Dionne

Don’t Make Me Over, the documentary featuring Warwick in her eighties, provides an intimate look at the elder “Queen of Twitter [x]” as a snug fit for the batty aunt of the internet—in the most endearing sense. For one, she refers to herself in third person by full name. Concerning gossip, she quips, “Talk about me…as long as you spell my name right!” Perhaps her response to the gossip she endured in a bygone era reveals that Warwick’s quirks have also found her infamy. Her television appearances during the 1990s increased her visibility, and the public demonstrated an overwhelming response to the new shift Warwick made from recording music to endorsing and hosting the Psychic Friends Network. As of 2023, there are still several hours of footage on YouTube of Warwick promoting the 1-(900) number, $3.99/min service in commercials and daytime talk show-like infomercials. Though Warwick would not say so, her son, David, characterized her involvement with the network as “tough times.” Indeed, her time as host would be marked by bankruptcy and an adverse effect on Warwick’s singing career; her involvement as spokesperson overshadowed her career as a singer. It is notable that recent retrospectives on Warwick’s career make little mention of her Psychic Friends era.

Perhaps in asking the potential gossiper to spell her name right, the batty aunt was just taking a minute to flash her street cred. Warwick’s documentary features her berating “so called gangsta rappers” over their limited use of vocabulary. In reference to women, their direction in black music became rife with misogynistic themes and lyrics. This now largely forgotten moment in Warwick’s life merits additional discussion. Who Warwick called “so-called” numbered none other than Snoop Dogg and Suge Knight, who both enjoy perennial respect and notoriety in hip-hop circles; at the time, they were treated like royalty and met with a warm reception at American contemporary radio. Warwick sought a way to forge a conversation with the new guard of black musicians and did so by infamously inviting the rappers and their entourages to her home at 7:00 AM for fellowship and conversation. Once they arrived, Warwick challenged Snoop Dogg to call her a “bitch.” Caught off guard, the rapper conceded, and eventually moderated misogynistic content on his subsequent studio album, The Doggfather (1996). For Warwick’s part, she would continue to leverage her celebrity against lyrical misogyny in a C- SPAN 1998 address of the National Congress of Black Women, lambasting hip-hop.

If Warwick found little critical love or fanfare from music critics for her music, she certainly was not going to find it by censuring a genre that performs black pain. Hip-hop became the critical darling of rock critics after rock. Due to the confrontation with Snoop Dogg and Suge Knight, Warwick would find few friends in other rappers. This is evident in Anthony DeCurtis’s 1997 piece on the Wu-Tang Clan, the hip-hop collective considered deities for their music, business acumen, and cultural impact. With individual achievements and success with the collective to his name, Wu-Tang member Method Man called Warwick out by name for wanting to stop “it;” it, meaning both the currents of contemporary black music—namely, hip-hop—as well as lyrical content. Choosing to stand against the general direction of black music, Warwick was well-intentioned, but perhaps out of step in her messaging of criticism towards hip-hop. In 2023, this rift is all but forgotten, as her single “Walk on By” became heavily sampled in Doja Cat’s “Paint the Town Red.” Raekwon’s words, said over a quarter a century ago, were prophetic: “You’ll never be able to stop this.” Dionne Warwick can be very much at home in hip-hop, despite the pervasiveness of explicit lyrics and content. After all, it was Snoop who admitted in early 2023 that he believed he’d been out-gangsta’d by Warwick’s gutsy confrontation. For her part, the matriarch never feared straightening out her unruly nephews and nieces.

It is this staunch, witty, and batty character that has slowly affected popular culture’s rendering of Dionne Warwick and her legacy. Memorable caricatures on Saturday Night Live and the cult YouTube series Got 2B Real feature Warwick as a deep-voiced elder stateswoman with little regard for censoring herself. The former takes Warwick’s peerless commentary on Twitter/x and creates a second Dionne Warwick talk show from her theatrical wisecracks online. The latter, a consummate parody of the classes of “divas” to which Warwick has belonged to and engaged, took archival footage of her and dubbed over her actual voice with a significantly deeper, molasses-like affectation that shows little regard for the antics of other divas, and specifically her rival, Aretha Franklin.

Warwick draws upon the same sass in her newest role, the “Queen of Twitter [x].” A fun, witty, sarcastic, and loving personality, Warwick enmeshed her years of expertise in the entertainment business to foment a renaissance in her perspective online. Recognized by this honorific title in 2020, Warwick has ascended to the summit of Twitter comedy by affectionately showing her age, as it were. She asks Twitter/x users to not explain to her what a “hot girl” is, despite the hordes of responses she received. She demanded performer Chance the Rapper explain the redundancy of his name and questioned the misspelling of Abel Tesfaye’s stage name (The Weeknd). There has been speculation that her niece Brittani is a stand-in for Warwick online, but here, the lore is greater than the logistics. Though Warwick’s genius on Twitter/x has been heralded in several publications, I fear her persona may have eclipsed her oeuvre.

Perhaps queering of Dionne Warwick can reintroduce audiences to her musicality and work, which continues to expand today. Though Warwick is a great female singer, she does lack an element of heterosexual desire. Throughout my research, I was hard-pressed to find ardor or passionate overtures towards Warwick besides the compliments given to her appearance (svelte, high cheekbones, statuesque). These niceties call her romantic relationships into question. The Oxford African American Studies Center recalls that in 1963, Warwick became Dionne Elliot with her marriage to drummer and actor William “Bill” Elliot II. That is the full extent of her reported romances; much must be mined elsewhere to find where Warwick is the object or perhaps, the instigator of desire. When asked what makes a woman feminine during her 1970s nadir, it may have been asked in the context of Warwick exuding very little heterosexual sexiness. She was indeed sensual throughout her career, but at a distance ordained by respectability and Christian social mores. There remains, however, the not-oft reflected time Warwick spent in Europe under the watchful (or playful) eye of Marlene Dietrich. Dietrich enjoyed whirlwind romances with women and men, including Burt Bacharach. Perhaps the arranger and Dietrich agreed to educate young Warwick in the customs of Parisian and European style and culture. Mentor? Sponsor? Lover? The possibilities loom as the idea of Warwick’s heterosexuality seem obligatory and flat.

Dionne Warwick: Final Notes

Dionne Warwick at her finest hour is hard to locate, given so many years of transcendent, powerful performances of a very rigorous repertoire. However, I nominate her live album Hot! Live and Otherwise (1981). Although Warwick had yet to record big hits like “Heartbreaker” and “That’s What Friends Are For,” the album was recorded right after her first reemergence, meaning that the Grammy-winning songs “I’ll Never Love This Way Again” and “Déjà vu” are present with the full strength of her 1960s catalogue. Recorded as a double album with five studio recordings, the album details a consistent artistry—by 1981, Warwick was not only talented and capable, but she had also crafted her own sound that remained throughout her newer material as well as her twenty-minute hit record medley. Her vocals are formidable and spectacular, the arrangements soaring higher and further than the originals. Specifically, “I’ll Never Love This Way Again” features a piercing climax that powerfully overtakes the 1979 original. To recognize and respect the career that Warwick survived until that point, to acknowledge her session work with the Gospelaires, the Sweet Inspirations, and the Drinkard Singers, to see her sing “Don’t Make Me Over” (1962) alone, but to know of her extensive work with partners Bacharach and David, to fight genre divisions and racial backlash, to see her temporarily down but not out, and to finally hear her triumphantly come back is the culmination of a retrospective on the makings of a successful career in the music business. That is Hot! Live and Otherwise.

On Warwick’s silken vocal presence alone, music audiences of all ages should return to Warwick’s music. Her oeuvre proffers the quality of a masterful vocalist. The following six songs, though not entirely my favorites, solidified my interest in Warwick as a significant vocalist of the modern rock era: “Walk on By” (1964) is marked by Warwick’s quietness as a point of dismissal to her lost lover. Now, the backdrop of Doja Cat’s Billboard Hot 100 #1 single, “Walk on By” undoubtedly finds new audiences within the sample-conscious black community. The teardrops running down the cheekbones of an otherwise dignified face can be heard within the song’s lyrics and Warwick’s delivery. With “Walk on By,” Warwick also participates in Black artistry, but from an interiority of sophistication and restraint—not from a desire to bowl audiences over with gratuitous vocal gymnastics. This would be her stance in much of her work, a cultivated elegance that created a path for other black vocalists, such as Luther Vandross, to find soul through softer means.

Live performances of “I’ll Never Love This Way Again” (1979) around 1979 – 82 feature Warwick’s smart use of lilt, the lifting and falling of her voice. However, her inflections are fitting, satisfying vocal flourishes that were deliberately not ostentatious. Rather, they adorn the song with notes that simultaneously disguise the melody she does not want to sing, and perform Warwick’s interiority, the source of the totality of her personhood. “Déjà vu,” the second single from Dionne (1979) showcases Warwick’s command over delicate phrasing. Live performances test the lower limits of voice, soft and silky. Her performance on Hot! Live and Otherwise sees Warwick make several audacious vocal choices, showing the versatility and elastic nature of her voice at that time.

Warwick’s live performance of “Battle Hymn of the Republic” (1968), mentioned earlier for its performance in honor of the death of MLK, portends a conscious Warwick, one who knows the gift of song in tragedy. With the recording of “What the World Needs Now” (1965), Warwick continued to see herself as a harbinger of messages of love, hope, and peace. The same can be said for her soul-stirring performance of Nina Simone’s “To Be Young, Gifted, and Black” (1972). The latter is perhaps the cornerstone of the Dionne Warwick Institute in East Orange, New Jersey, where Warwick was born, and where over 350 young, gifted, and black students learn. Perhaps learned herself from her years within the church, Warwick would later use her music to benefit the advancement of AIDS Research. But these early performances are poignant—they show the singer’s humanity, dignified and uncharacteristically transparent.

Celebrity and artistry are often diametric opposites. Often the adulation a celebrity receives for who they are can detract attention from what they do. The cultural products an artist produces may be eclipsed for the notoriety they may exude in-process. Dionne Warwick continually regained stature throughout her life in the spotlight, but perhaps at the cost of younger generations believing her to be synonymous with psychic grifts or Twitter beefs with Chance the Rapper before knowing of her accomplishments on the Pop Charts in the sixties, seventies and eighties. With weathered voice, Warwick the celebrity continues to perform. But in that voice is an artist’s testament to resilience. For her efforts, her significant contributions made to black female vocality and to American popular music, Warwick the artist has become a perennial favorite of many, and long deserved the Kennedy Center Honors she finally received in 2023. Though, at eighty-three, she shows no signs of stopping at all, she may rest on her laurels and tweet a link to “Anyone Who Had a Heart” (1964) or “Alfie” (1967) or maybe even “Do You Know the Way to San Jose?” (1968). Still reigning, the Queen’s adoring public waits for the next witty remark. We wouldn’t have it any other way.
