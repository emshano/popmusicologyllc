A Lamb’s Ode to “Loverboy”

“Loverboy (Remix)” Mariah Carey, Glitter (2001)

I’m not sure why, but too many years elapsed before Mariah Carey would even acknowledge Glitter (2001). Considered a misfire by most outside of her fandom, mentions of “Loverboy” and Glitter were met with derision and jokes about her mental health. Granted, Glitter was an ambitious reimagining of Mariah’s life story-cum-A Star is Born at the intersection of 2001 Pop and 1983 Funk. For many critics, “ambitious” was considered an understatement: Carey’s vehicle crashed, both the box office and in critics’ hearts. For this lamb, who attempts to moonlight as a critic, I cannot help but make sense of the wreck, because context helps me to understand, and once I do, I get to enjoy.

The Glitter soundtrack’s poor commercial performance reflected a tepid reception to Mariah Carey at the time. What’s worse, Carey’s highly documented mental exhaustion, coupled with her arguably stiff portrayal of Billie Frank allowed many to seize the opportunity to censure the project as a near-fatal failure—misguided, melodramatic, and spectacularly overblown. Indeed, Carey’s sharp commercial recession dovetailed well with America’s acute need for cathartic laughs, and so, the singer would become the comedic distraction from other, critical events at the time. Glitter was released on September 11th, 2001.

Now, nearly a quarter century after the fact, pop gold can be mined from this soundtrack as an artifact of a bygone era. For the artist, for music historians like me, and for fans of pop history, such retrospection towards pop’s flops allows us to examine the lasting ways that works like Glitter make their impact, even if it isn’t at the summit of the Billboard 200. An instant cult classic, now finding its footing as a work of kitsch, its accompanying soundtrack and its four singles represent an adventurous mélange of pop, funk, hip-hop and R&B, fused together with harmonious fun that few other people than Mariah Carey are capable of.

Take the album’s first single. The “Loverboy (Remix) begins with a “pop,” and the track hits with a firecracker spark that explodes into a charged barrage of lusty come-ons between raunchy rappers. Mariah enters the melee, singing in delirious ecstasy over how good her lover makes her feel. The pop song equivalent of the best parts of mania, the “Loverboy (Remix)” crams hypersexuality, patriotic imagery, thinly veiled jabs at Mariah’s rival Jennifer Lopez, drag racing, hub caps, and a cavalcade of rappers in four-and-a-half minutes of nonstop thrill.

Pushing the envelope, “Loverboy” is far beyond the threshold of earlier chart-topping hits in Carey’s catalogue. Much like Lux Lisbon, the amorous anti-hero from Jeffrey Eugenides’ The Virgin Suicides, Carey’s first outing for Virgin was anything but. The demure quality of “Fantasy,” “Honey,” and “Heartbreaker,” were shed on the fun, funky, and suggestive “Loverboy”. Carey’s newfound sexual candor took centerstage as she crooned and frothily cooed about her lover, her sugar daddy, how his lovin’ stoked her insatiability.

Sonically, Carey was committed as ever to the world of the amusement park. High-noted whees recall the titillation of a rapidly descending rollercoaster, and the first staccato “ahh” at the album’s conclusion featuring Cameo are the closest thing to a Mariah Carey Pepsi commercial, if she didn’t make her own six years later. Engineered to be a literal blast from end to end, “Loverboy” resulted from Carey ‘s collaboration with record producer Clark Kent. Their mission? To replace “Loverboy’s” old sample—Yellow Magic Orchestra’s “Firecracker”—with the sound of Cameo’s “Candy”, thus creating a jauntier take on the 1983 song.

Carey’s hazy female lead vocal is introduced by guest rappers Da Brat, Ludacris, 22 and Shawna. Though at the time, many claimed Carey had been drowned out in a crowded song, Carey is participating in a hip-hop precedent which is now normal practice in popular music. I can immediately think of the lengthy list of collaborators on My Beautiful Dark Twisted Fantasy (2010), Beyoncé (2013), and Renaissance (2022) to name a few.

With minimal radio exposure, “Loverboy” holds the dubious honor of breaking Mariah’s #1 streak by peaking at the penultimate on the Hot 100, strategically held from #1 by Destiny’s Child’s “Bootylicious”. Nevertheless, the hit resulted from Virgin Records executives slashing the price of the single by 83% and the widespread frenzy that fomented around speculations of Carey’s illness. Selling the song for 49 cents catapulted the song up 58 positions, a tectonic movement then that’s commonplace on today’s charts. Though Mariah and Virgin were derided for “manipulating” the charts, most songs are sold for the same price today.

Today, artists as sexually permissive as Peaches or Kim Petras make Mariah’s verses seem passé. But in context, the raciness of Mariah’s lyrics, “His loving takes me high/ like a taste of ecstasy/all I need for him is to be my lover boy,” risked leveraging Carey’s maturation to pique curiosity. Despite lukewarm success, “Loverboy” pays cultural dividends. Its salaciousness, never seen by a singer of Carey’s pedigree, was propelled by her erotic singing, giving inspiration and rationale to a cadre of younger female musicians singing authentically about sexuality. Ariana Grande’s ode to the romp in “Side to Side” could not be conceived without “Loverboy.” The song expanded the expressive range for women to include their sexual voracity.

For being at the cutting edge of several breakthroughs in popular music (sonic, strategic, and scintillating), “Loverboy” merits a deserving second listen. It is the harbinger of a paradigm shift in the sale and promotion of popular singles, a foretelling the tale of pop’s further entanglement with hip-hop in the 2000s, and America’s fodder when the country lived through a waking nightmare.

Its muddled release aside, “Loverboy” boasts genius writing, incomparable singing, and lurid imagery. Lambs—the name Carey gave to her fanbase—have more ways to celebrate “Loverboy” today than during its original chart run. Thanks to a lamb-led #justiceforGlitter campaign in 2018, Glitter mounted an impressive comeback. So too should its Hip-Hop/Funk-Pop lead single be revisited for its triumphs.
