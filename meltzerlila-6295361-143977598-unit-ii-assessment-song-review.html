Unit II Assessment - Song Review

Blind Lemon Jefferson’s “That Black Snake Moan” exemplifies the raw, expressive style of country blues. As a secular genre rooted in African American folk traditions, country blues captured the struggles and experiences of Black musicians in the post-emancipation era (Ocran). Released in 1927, the song reflects this history—shaping the genre through oral tradition, regional influences, and Jefferson’s distinctive musical innovations.

In "That Black Snake Moan," Jefferson employs metaphorical language that speaks to personal struggle and emotional distress. The lyrics follow the common blues composition three-line stanza structure, where the first line is repeated twice before a responding third line (Ocran). The central metaphor of the "black snake" serves as a powerful symbol and follows the blues tradition of using imagery to convey deeper meanings about personal and collective experiences (Ocran). This creative approach to storytelling reflects how blues texts "voice, contemplate, and musically embody Black alternatives to white supremacy” (Ocran).

Musically, the song’s foundation demonstrates the genre's characteristic blend of African and European traditions. Similarly, Motown would later combine "elements from rhythm and blues, gospel, and rock and roll" (Crawford & Hamberlin 463). The instrumental arrangement centers on Jefferson's fingerpicking guitar work to create a rich, layered sound. The song follows a loosely structured blues form, deviating from the standardized 12-bar blues pattern by incorporating irregular phrasing (01:12). This structural flexibility reflects the oral tradition of blues, where songs evolved with each performance rather than adhering to rigid musical conventions (Ocran).

Jefferson’s vocal delivery is raw and emotive, featuring his signature high-pitched moans that add to the song’s haunting quality (00:35). His voice, almost wailing at times, enhances the song’s melancholic atmosphere, a technique deeply rooted in African American field hollers and work songs (Ocran). His noteworthy guitar playing demonstrates the traditional West African "call and response" pattern between voice and instrument (Ocran). The performance reflects the "racially insular development of blues across the Southeast," where musicians developed highly individualized styles within their cultural context (Ocran).

In a contemporary setting, “That Black Snake Moan” remains particularly relevant for historical retrospectives on blues and its history. The song emerged during a period of significant demographic shifts that would eventually lead to the urban blues movement (Ocran). It could also serve as inspiration for modern acoustic blues artists who seek to emulate Jefferson’s emotive style. Given its themes of longing and despair, it could be featured in film soundtracks or curated playlists exploring early American music’s influence on rock, folk, and country (Ocran). Much like how rhythm and blues "owed much to broadcasting" for reaching diverse audiences, modern streaming platforms and music education programs can help new generations discover and appreciate Jefferson's pioneering artistry (Crawford & Hamberlin 423).
