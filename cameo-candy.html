Student writing · Track 10

Cameo — Candy

STUDENT WRITINGOpen original · Google Docs

“Candy,” a 1986 song by the American funk band Cameo, is a track that exemplifies the 1980s funk sound. While its syncopated bass line, mind-numbing chorus, and free-feeling lead vocals place it into the funk genre, its electronic synths are undeniably 1980s. This is supported by the track’s success on the Billboard during its release and its contemporary identity as a 1980s throwback track.

Musical Elements

At a length of approximately five and a half minutes, “Candy” is underscored by an unceasing, syncopated bass riff, which persists even through the instrumental break and sax solo that takes place halfway through the song. The track begins with a melody borrowed from its chorus (“It’s like candy” at 0:19), which is the anchoring phrase of the song. Throughout the song, short vocal runs are also spaced out and provide variety to the catchy and repeating chorus. This is undoubtedly a song that encourages dance and movement, as described in the extramusical elements section.

On top of the bass riffs are 80s-style electronic synths, drums, saxophone, and electric guitar. The lead vocalist, Larry Blackmon, has a voice that is reminiscent of Black vocalism from decades past (e.g. the blues of the 1930s) — with a loud and passionate, yet imperfect and free-feeling sound (as opposed to, say, more rigid genres like choir music). Nonetheless, it feels like the Blackmon and the background singers share the same amount of time in this song due to the strong use of call-and-response sections, another element which suggests this song’s African American musical roots.

Lyrically, this song is quite simple. The group sings in a first-person, singular voice to describe their feelings towards an unspecified subject. This subject, likely cocaine (i.e. nose candy), gives the lyrical voice “a heart attack / … the kind I like” (2:22), “takes over” him (0:21), and makes him “toss and turn” (1:13) in bed. It is compared to “strawberries, blueberries, all those good things” (3:43) and makes his eyes “roll” in his head when it is “starting,” (1:08), alluding to a high. The song follows an upbeat tempo of 111 beats per minute and 4/4 time signature, which makes the song feel celebratory and light.

Extramusical Elements

Out of all the songs that I’ve reviewed in this course, this track aligns the best with my personal music tastes — likely due to the fact that its sound is the most contemporary. Compared to the blues song I reviewed in part two and the Motown girl group song in part three, this song feels like it has the most relevance to pop music today. As we have discussed in class, “Candy” has been heavily sampled decades after its release, with a prominent vocal and musical feature in a version of “Loverboy” by Mariah Carey in 2001.

While “Candy” doesn’t feel like a song that any mainstream pop artist would release today, I see connections with more contemporary tracks that are centered around heavy bass lines and strong, catchy choruses, such as “Lie to Me” by 5 Seconds of Summer, “Houdini” by Dua Lipa, and to a lesser extent, “Watermelon Sugar” by Harry Styles.

“Candy” is by all means a dancing song, with its accompanying music video featuring members of Cameo grooving out with unnamed women in front of blurry, psychedelic-esque shots of 1980s Times Square (Cameo). Today, I think it fits best as a “throwback” song of sorts, either in the queue of an 80s radio station, repurposed as a TikTok (not a dance, but as a background track), or resampled. It holds its relevance today, but is recognizably from a bygone era.

Historical and Contextual Elements

Given its funk identity, “Candy” is a direct result of the post-soul developments in African American music of the 1960s and 70s. For example, when comparing “Candy” to older funk songs like Sly and the Family Stone’s 1969 “Everyday People” one hears sonic similarities: the raspiness of the lead vocals, the focus on a remarkable chorus, the use of saxophone, a strong bass line, and heavily layered backing vocals. It is undeniably funk.

Nonetheless, “Candy” is representative of the technological advances of the 70s and 80s, with light electronic synths and digital-sounding percussion instrumentation throughout the track (Reynolds). This is an innovation that was not seen even in Cameo’s own older work, such as their 1979 song “I Just Want to Be.” As opposed to the funk of years past or the soul music which preceded it (e.g. 1960s Marvin Gaye), “Candy” is more contemporary sounding.

During its original release, “Candy” reached #1 on the R&B charts and #21 on the Billboard Top 100, demonstrating its faithful representation of 1980s listeners’ tastes across genres (Whitburn). Today, it has been featured in numerous media, used in several contemporary films as background music for scenes of wedding dances (Corradi) and roller skating (Tavares and Kaluuya), thus reaffirming its current identity as a throwback, celebratory song.

One might see the foreshadowing of 1990s music in this track, given that in the following decade, electronic instruments became more affordable and widely used. Songs like this, which fetishize and celebrate drug use, also may have contributed to continual anti-drug and anti-crime cultural panic of the 1990s (Johnston).
